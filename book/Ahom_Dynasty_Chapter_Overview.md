# Ahom Dynasty — Chapter and Content Overview

Main narrative: 1228–1828. Detailed plan: Ahom_Dynasty_Book_Plan_1228_to_Gomdhar_Konwar.md.


## Part I — Sources, origins, and the early kingdom

1. **How We Know Ahom History** (Method and sources): Buranjis in Tai-Ahom and Assamese: authorship, manuscript families, copying, translation, and court purposes. Inscriptions, copper plates, coins, archaeology, diplomatic letters, religious literature, genealogies, and material culture. Persian, Burmese, British, and other external accounts: what each can and cannot establish. Colonial, nationalist, regional, community, and recent academic interpretations; missing voices and archival silences. Rules for separating documented events, probable reconstructions, oral traditions, legends, and modern commemorative claims.

2. **The Brahmaputra Valley on the Eve of 1228** (Background to 1228): River systems, floodplains, forests, hill passages, wet-rice zones, and travel constraints. Political landscape after the fragmentation of Kamarupa; distinguish later state boundaries from medieval ones. Moran, Borahi, Chutiya, Kachari/Dimasa, Bhuyan, and other communities: their own histories and changing relationships. Existing settlement, cultivation, exchange, religion, and languages before Ahom arrival. How geography shaped the possibilities for state formation, warfare, and diplomacy.

3. **Tai Origins and Royal Ancestry** (Before 1228; introductory background): Tai-speaking worlds, Mong Mao and related regional polities, migration traditions, and historical geography. Sukaphaa’s ancestry, titles, companions, and variant genealogies; document what is uncertain. Khunlung–Khunlai and divine-origin narratives as traditions of legitimacy rather than dated political fact. Language, ritual specialists, agricultural knowledge, and forms of leadership carried into Assam. Relationship between Tai-Ahom identity, the royal dynasty, and the later multi-community kingdom.

4. **Across the Patkai: Arrival in 1228** (Journey and arrival): Competing accounts of departure, migration stages, route, companions, animals, and dates. Patkai crossings and encounters with frontier communities, including both negotiation and violence where supported. Arrival in the Brahmaputra valley and the conventional significance of 1228. Settlement choices, river crossings, scouting, food supply, and adaptation. Identify later heroic additions and unsupported numerical claims in popular accounts.

5. **Sukaphaa and the Founding Kingdom** (1228–1268; working chronology): Settlement phases, the establishment of Charaideo, and the growth of the first court. Moran and Borahi relations: alliances, intermarriage, conflict, incorporation, and local agency. Early institutions, leading officers, land, rice cultivation, labour, and royal household. The foundations of kingship, ritual life, succession, and historical record keeping. Sukaphaa’s death, succession, funerary traditions, and the difference between contemporary evidence and later memory.

6. **The First Successors: Suteuphaa, Subinphaa, and Sukhaangphaa** (1268–1332; working chronology): Individual reign dossiers for Suteuphaa, Subinphaa, and Sukhaangphaa, including alternate spellings. Early territorial consolidation and relations with neighbouring rulers and communities. Royal lineages, clan traditions, nobles, and succession arrangements. Cultivation, population incorporation, and development of settlements and offices. Short notices and uncertain episodes retained in an appendix rather than inflated into invented narratives.

7. **Succession Crises and Rule without a King** (1332–1397; working chronology): Sukhrangphaa, Sutuphaa, and Tyao Khamti: separate biographies and reign narratives. Political roles of nobles, royal households, and competing claimants. Interregna: who exercised authority, why a ruler was not installed, and what sources say. Assassination traditions, external encounters, and chronology disagreements. Institutional continuity despite disputed succession; avoid assuming the state ceased to exist during an interregnum.

8. **Sudangphaa: The Bamuni Konwar** (1397–1407; working chronology): Childhood and upbringing traditions, recovery of royal identity, and accession. Brahmanical influence, court ritual, royal legitimacy, and limits of claims about an abrupt religious conversion. Noble opposition and the political consolidation of the reign. Frontier diplomacy, campaigns, and agreements attributed to this ruler. Death, succession, and the later significance of the Bamuni Konwar story.

9. **The Fifteenth-Century Kingdom** (1407–1497; working chronology): Individual sections for Sujangphaa, Suphakphaa, Susenphaa, Suhenphaa, and Supimphaa. Frontier relations, campaigns, internal conflict, and succession changes under each ruler. Settlement, cultivation, administrative growth, and integration of communities. Noble families, royal kinship, court violence, and competing historical explanations. A complete catalogue of smaller recorded events and gaps in surviving documentation.


## Part II — Expansion and the sixteenth-century regional order

10. **Suhungmung and a Larger State** (1497–1539; working chronology): Accession, royal names and titles, household, court, and political ambitions. Territorial growth as a series of campaigns and negotiations, not one uniform conquest. Administrative innovations, regional offices, settlement, and incorporation of new populations. Changes in royal language, titles, religious patronage, and political identity. Opposition, succession struggle, death, and an assessment of the reign through multiple source traditions.

11. **The Chutiya Kingdom and Ahom Expansion** (Early sixteenth century): Chutiya political history and institutions before incorporation; include Chutiya perspectives. Campaign sequence, rulers, commanders, locations, and disputed dates. Traditions concerning Sati Sadhani/Sadhini: distinguish documentary history, oral tradition, and commemoration. Sadiya administration, population movements, craft knowledge, religious sites, and military resources. Resistance, accommodation, and long-term changes after conquest.

12. **Ahom–Kachari/Dimasa Relations** (Sixteenth century, with necessary background): Kachari/Dimasa political geography and the significance of Dimapur and later centres. Campaigns, commanders, diplomatic demands, settlements, and relocations. Weapons, forts, river routes, and limits of battlefield reconstructions. Consequences for trade, frontier control, population, and administration. Distinguish the histories of neighbouring peoples from a narrative told only from the Ahom court.

13. **Bengal Campaigns, Turbak, and Mula Gabharu** (Early sixteenth century): Bengal’s political context and the sequence of invasions and counter-campaigns. Turbak, Ahom commanders, military adaptations, and the battles recorded in different accounts. Mula Gabharu’s life, actions, source history, and later representation. Captives, settlement, craft skills, religious communities, and the aftermath of warfare. Check famous speeches, casualty totals, battle locations, and claims about the origins of institutions.

14. **Suklenmung and Garhgaon** (1539–1552; working chronology): Accession, lineage, major officers, and the structure of the court. The development of Garhgaon: landscape, defence, settlement, and royal space. Campaigns, frontier consolidation, roads, and other works attributed to the reign. Coins, inscriptions, titles, and religious patronage where surviving evidence permits. Death, succession, and links between the expanded state and its next phase.

15. **Sukhaamphaa and the Koch Challenge** (1552–1603; working chronology): The long reign and the Khora Raja tradition; royal kinship and principal officers. Nara Narayan, Chilarai, Koch power, and the sequence of Ahom–Koch confrontations. Defeat, terms of settlement, hostages, tribute, and political recovery. Territorial limits, frontier security, alliances, and the later division of Koch power. Long-term consequences for relations with Bengal and the emerging Mughal frontier.

16. **Diplomacy and the Regional Order before 1603** (Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries; synthesis): Comparative relations with Kamata/Koch, Chutiya, Kachari/Dimasa, Bhuyans, and eastern Tai polities. Envoys, marriage, gifts, tribute, hostage arrangements, written agreements, and ceremonial ranking. Markets, passes, buffer zones, and the difference between tribute and direct administration. Periodic conflict and negotiation with hill and frontier communities; terminology must be historically explained. A source-backed register of every recoverable diplomatic mission before 1603.


## Part III — Mughal wars and the succession crisis

17. **Pratap Singha and the Reorganisation of Power** (1603–1641; working chronology): Accession of Susenghphaa/Pratap Singha and the priorities of the reign. Momai Tamuli and other leading administrators; the evolution of the Borbarua and Borphukan offices. Reorganisation of labour, settlements, provincial administration, and military command. Royal policy towards religious institutions and the changing political economy. Court politics, frontier policy, succession, and the limits of claims assigning every institution to one reformer.

18. **The First Major Ahom–Mughal War Cycle** (Approximately 1615–1639): Mughal expansion through Bengal, Koch Hajo, local allies, and frontier ambitions. Campaign-by-campaign accounts of early fighting, including engagements such as Samdhara where supported. Balinarayan and regional resistance; commanders, forts, river crossings, and supply. Compare Ahom chronicles with Mirza Nathan’s Baharistan-i-Ghaybi and other Persian accounts. The Asurar Ali settlement, its terms, frontier implications, and later violations.

19. **The Short Reigns after Pratap Singha** (1641–1648; working chronology): Suramphaa/Jayaditya Singha and Sutingphaa: individual reign and household dossiers. Accession, ministers, royal disputes, illness traditions, deposition, and succession. Continuity and change in administration, religious patronage, and frontier policy. What chronicles record and what later authors infer from sparse material. How short reigns affected the court before Jayadhwaj Singha’s accession.

20. **Jayadhwaj Singha before the Invasion** (1648–1663; focus before 1662): Sutamla/Jayadhwaj Singha: accession, officers, household, and religious patronage. Western expansion, Mughal weakness, Guwahati, and the disputed frontier. Military and administrative preparations, alliances, and intelligence. Vaishnava affiliations and the coexistence of different religious practices. The political circumstances that preceded Mir Jumla’s campaign.

21. **Mir Jumla’s Invasion** (1662–1663): Mughal objectives, commanders, routes, boats, artillery, and logistics. Ahom defensive decisions, retreat, the occupation of the capital, and resistance. Monsoon, disease, supply failure, communications, and civilian experiences. Comparison of Ahom records, Persian narratives, and later historical writing. Separate attested movements from dramatic reconstructions of conversations or motives.

22. **Ghilajharighat and Ramani Gabharu** (1663 and its aftermath): Treaty negotiations, payments, hostages, boundaries, and obligations as recorded in relevant texts. The political and economic consequences for the royal household and kingdom. Ramani Gabharu: kinship, transfer to the Mughal court, later life, and limits of the evidence. Diplomatic correspondence and marriage as instruments of imperial politics. Jayadhwaj’s death, succession, and how the settlement was remembered.

23. **Chakradhwaj Singha and the Recovery of Guwahati** (1663–1670; working chronology): Supangmung/Chakradhwaj Singha: accession and the decision to resist Mughal demands. Lachit Borphukan, Atan Burhagohain, ministers, officers, and collective command. Recruitment, shipbuilding, fortification, supplies, and the recovery campaign. Recapture of Guwahati and rebuilding of defensive positions. Assess royal speeches, command legends, and claims about individual responsibility against their earliest sources.

24. **Alaboi: Defeat and Its Consequences** (1669): Political and military pressure surrounding the confrontation. Terrain, deployment, command choices, and the limits of tactical reconstruction. The battle sequence and variations in casualty figures. Consequences for strategy, morale, fortification, negotiations, and civilian communities. How Alaboi entered memory and why defeat belongs alongside victory in a complete history.

25. **Saraighat: War on the Brahmaputra** (1671): Ram Singh’s campaign, negotiations, river defences, and the strategic position of Guwahati. Lachit, Atan, naval officers, soldiers, labourers, allies, and opposing commanders. Land engagements, naval manoeuvres, supply, illness traditions, and battle chronology. Udayaditya Singha’s reign during the battle and the wider political setting. Victory, its limits, subsequent frontier developments, and the source history of famous anecdotes.

26. **After Saraighat: Udayaditya and Ramadhwaj** (1670–1674; overlapping battle context): Sunyatphaa/Udayaditya Singha and Suklamphaa/Ramadhwaj Singha: accession, household, and officers. Religious disputes, court coalitions, royal removals, and political violence. Atan Burhagohain, Debera Borbarua, and competing centres of authority. The transition from military success to succession crisis. Explain overlap with the Saraighat chapter using cross-references instead of repeating the battle.

27. **Kingmakers and the Rapid Succession Crisis** (1674–1679; working chronology): Suhung, Gobar, Sujinphaa, and Sudoiphaa: complete short-reign sections. Debera Borbarua, Atan Burhagohain, Laluk Sola Borphukan, and shifting alliances. Installations, assassinations, ministerial purges, and disputed chronology. The loss of Guwahati and its political and military circumstances. Effects on provincial command, labour mobilisation, court trust, and ordinary inhabitants.

28. **Sulikphaa, Joymoti, and Gadapani** (1679–1681; working chronology): Sulikphaa/Ratnadhwaj Singha and the Lora Raja tradition: accession and ruling coalition. Treatment of royal rivals and the rules and practices surrounding eligibility for kingship. Joymoti’s biography, Gadapani’s movements, persecution traditions, and sources for the narrative. Noble opposition, the change of ruler, and the creation of a new political settlement. Compare documentary accounts with later theatre, cinema, literature, and memorial culture.


## Part IV — Tungkhungia restoration and eighteenth-century royal power

29. **Gadadhar Singha and the Battle of Itakhuli** (1681–1696; battle focus 1682): Supaatphaa/Gadadhar Singha: accession and the Tungkhungia ruling line. Restoration of command, ministerial power, labour obligations, and political stability. The Itakhuli campaign, recapture of Guwahati, and the western frontier settlement. Religious policy, public works, punishment, and social consequences. Succession, assessment of the reign, and comparisons with earlier recoveries.

30. **Rudra Singha: Royal Ambition and Cultural Expansion** (1696–1714; working chronology): Sukhrungphaa/Rudra Singha: court, ministers, royal family, and political ambitions. Rangpur, Joysagar, patronage, workshops, and the development of royal infrastructure. Relations with Kachari/Dimasa, Jaintia, neighbouring rulers, and religious institutions. Projected wider campaigns: distinguish preparation, intention, and operations actually undertaken. Death, succession plans, court culture, and burdens of labour and expenditure.

31. **Siva Singha and the Bar Rajas** (1714–1744; working chronology): Sutanphaa/Siva Singha: accession, court, officers, and religious patronage. Phuleswari, Ambika, and Sarbeswari: individual biographies, authority, titles, and succession. Temples, tanks, gifts, inscriptions, royal ritual, and the organisation of patronage. Relations with Vaishnava communities and the source history of coercion and humiliation narratives. Connect tensions to later developments while avoiding a single-cause explanation of rebellion.

32. **Pramatta Singha: Construction and Consolidation** (1744–1751; working chronology): Sunenphaa/Pramatta Singha: accession, household, principal officers, and court politics. Rang Ghar and other attributed projects: construction phases, inscriptions, materials, and function. Continuity in regional policy, administration, religion, and labour mobilisation. Festivities, royal display, craftsmanship, and the social costs of monumental building. Death and succession; distinguish historical structures from later restoration.

33. **Rajeshwar Singha: Court, Campaigns, and Pressure** (1751–1769; working chronology): Suremphaa and Rajeshwar Singha: names, accession, household, and ministers. Kirtichandra Borbarua and court power; evaluate traditions of chronicle suppression or destruction. Manipur relations, campaigns, political marriages, and the position of Kuranganayani. Palaces, temples, tanks, administration, and changing military and fiscal demands. Religious conflict, succession, and the conditions inherited by Lakshmi Singha.

34. **Neighbours, Frontiers, and Long-Distance Diplomacy** (1228–1769; thematic synthesis): Dedicated sections for Kachari/Dimasa, Jaintia, Koch polities, Manipur, Tripura, and relevant eastern connections. Relations with Naga, Mising/Miri, Mishmi, Khamti, Singpho, Bhutanese, and other communities where period evidence exists. Duar and posa arrangements, markets, gifts, tribute, marriage, envoys, and border disputes. Frontiers as negotiated spaces: distinguish claims, influence, occupation, tribute, and administration. Catalogue missions and agreements missed by a reign-by-reign narrative; date institutions individually.


## Part V — Government, society, economy, and cultural life

35. **Kingship, Government, and Justice** (1228–1828; developments by period): Royal succession, legitimacy, coronation, council politics, and limits on royal power. Burhagohain, Borgohain, Borpatrogohain, Borbarua, Borphukan, and subordinate officers. Provincial, local, frontier, household, and specialised departments and offices. Judicial practice, petitions, customary rules, punishments, property disputes, and evidence gaps. Appointments, service, gifts, royal orders, land grants, and institutional change over six centuries.

36. **The Paik System: Service, Land, and Obligation** (Evolution across the Ahom period): Origins and successive reforms; distinguish early practices from later standardisation. Paik categories, gots, khels, service rotation, officer hierarchy, and exemptions. Links between land, cultivation, military duty, transport, construction, and craft labour. Household burdens, displacement, desertion, unequal obligations, and dealings with religious institutions. Breakdown, adaptation, and the system’s relationship to rebellion and colonial transition.

37. **Army, Navy, Intelligence, and Fortifications** (Development across the Ahom period): Recruitment, command, infantry, boats, horses, elephants, artillery, and changing weapons. Shipbuilding, metalwork, gunpowder, workshops, procurement, and captured technology. River warfare, earthworks, stockades, garhs, defensive systems, and campaigns on different terrain. Scouting, information networks, diplomatic intelligence, logistics, disease, and supply. Surrender, captives, booty, military labour, and the civilian costs of warfare.

38. **Land, Agriculture, and the Rural Economy** (1228–1828): Wet rice, dry cultivation, irrigation, clearing, livestock, fisheries, and regional differences. Land categories, service land, grants, transfers, obligations, and tenure disputes. Village organisation, seasonal work, food storage, taxation, and state extraction. Floods, erosion, famine, epidemic, war damage, and recovery where documented. Change over time and the limits of reconstructing yields, incomes, or population from scattered sources.

39. **Trade, Money, Markets, and Craft Production** (1228–1828): Markets, river routes, roads, passes, frontier exchange, customs posts, and intermediaries. Coins, mints, inscriptions, metal standards, barter, and changes in monetisation. Silk, cotton, weaving, iron, brass, bell-metal, pottery, boats, arms, and other attested crafts. Court workshops, specialist khels, merchants, imports, exports, and labour relations. The economic effects of war, rebellion, early Company intervention, and shifting trading networks.

40. **Communities, Migration, and Social Hierarchy** (1228–1828): Ahom identity and the kingdom’s diverse populations; avoid treating dynasty, language, and ethnicity as interchangeable. Ahomisation, Assamese-language expansion, incorporation, intermarriage, and continuing distinct identities. Caste, rank, clan, profession, noble households, service groups, slavery, and dependency. Muslim communities, immigrants, captives, religious networks, and frontier mobility. Present Ahom, Moran, Borahi, Chutiya, Dimasa, Koch, Mising, Matak, and other perspectives where source support exists.

41. **Women, Family, and Political Agency** (1228–1828): Royal women, political marriages, succession, property, household authority, and court patronage. Case studies of Mula Gabharu, Ramani Gabharu, Joymoti, Phuleswari, Ambika, Sarbeswari, and Kuranganayani. Ordinary women’s cultivation, weaving, trade, ritual, household labour, and experiences of conflict. Marriage, kinship, inheritance, sexuality, widowhood, captivity, and status as far as evidence permits. Identify the limits imposed by male court authors and avoid presenting exceptional royal lives as universal experience.

42. **Religion, Ritual, and Religious Institutions** (1228–1828): Tai-Ahom cosmology, ancestor traditions, ritual specialists, ceremonies, and changing royal practice. Shaiva, Shakta, Vaishnava, and other religious traditions and their regional institutions. Sankardeva, Madhavdeva, Ekasarana traditions, sattras, namghars, and their wider social influence. Patronage, grants, labour exemptions, debate, conflict, and accommodation between court and religious authorities. Date changes carefully and distinguish living traditions from practices demonstrated for a specific medieval century.

43. **Languages, Literature, Education, and Historical Memory** (1228–1828, with later reception labelled): Tai-Ahom language and script, Assamese, Sanskrit, Persian contacts, and multilingual records. Buranji production, scribes, archives, translation, circulation, and manuscript preservation. Poetry, religious writing, drama, biography, technical texts, and court-sponsored learning. Religious institutions, family instruction, literacy, libraries, and knowledge transmission. Language change, genealogical memory, lost manuscripts, and later editing; distinguish original wording from modern translation.

44. **Capitals, Architecture, Roads, and Waterworks** (1228–1828): Charaideo, Garhgaon, Rangpur, Jorhat, and temporary centres: dates, purposes, movement, and landscape. Kareng Ghar, Talatal Ghar, Rang Ghar, temples, tanks, bridges, roads, forts, and provincial monuments. Royal and religious patronage, design, materials, artisans, engineering, and labour. Inscriptions, archaeological records, construction phases, damage, rebuilding, and mistaken attributions. Evaluate famous claims about secret tunnels, construction recipes, and architectural influence with evidence.

45. **Moidams, Objects, Art, and Royal Death** (1228–1828; modern conservation separately labelled): Funerary architecture, Charaideo and other sites, royal genealogy, burial and cremation practices. Ritual, grave goods, cosmology, changing construction, and archaeology of funerary landscapes. Coins, seals, weapons, regalia, sculpture, textiles, manuscripts, and museum objects. What excavation can establish and what requires interpretation; source criticism for human-sacrifice claims. Later damage, excavation, preservation, community custodianship, and a short clearly modern heritage note.

46. **Everyday Life, Environment, and Practical Knowledge** (1228–1828): Housing, food, clothing, ornaments, markets, leisure, performance, festivals, and regional variation. Agricultural calendars, astronomy, calendrical systems, medicine, veterinary knowledge, and technical manuscripts. Floods, river shifts, forests, disease, animals, and environmental constraints. The lives of cultivators, craftspeople, soldiers, servants, captives, children, and mobile communities. Separate historical descriptions from modern customs and acknowledge topics for which evidence remains sparse.


## Part VI — Rebellion, fragmentation, and foreign occupation

47. **Why the Moamoria Rebellion Became Possible** (Long background to 1769): Mayamora/Moamoria religious networks, social composition, and regional bases. Labour obligations, elite power, patronage, inequalities, and religious tensions. Court actions and reported incidents: compare sectarian and royal narratives. The roles of caste, rank, ethnic affiliation, personal conflict, and regional politics. An explicit comparison of competing explanations rather than assigning the rebellion one cause.

48. **Lakshmi Singha and the First Moamoria Uprising** (1769–1780; working chronology): Sunyeophaa/Lakshmi Singha: accession, ministers, household, and political vulnerability. Rebellion leadership, mobilisation, capture of power, rival authority, and royal displacement. Ramakanta, Raghav, Kuranganayani, and other actors with biographies tied to evidence. Counter-mobilisation, restoration, punishment, and effects on labour and cultivation. Chronology of suppression, renewed conflict, and the conditions inherited by Gaurinath.

49. **Gaurinath Singha and Prolonged Civil War** (1780–1795; working chronology): Suhitpangphaa/Gaurinath Singha, noble factions, and the renewal and spread of conflict. Rebel leaders, administrations, military fronts, and changes in control. Royal flight, provincial disorder, loss of manpower, and disruption of revenue and farming. Purnananda Burhagohain, the development of Jorhat, and political survival. Distinguish phases and regional movements that compressed summaries often combine into one uprising.

50. **Captain Welsh and the First Company Intervention** (1792–1794; working chronology): Appeals for assistance and the Company’s political, military, and commercial interests. Welsh’s expedition, route, operations, local alliances, and negotiations. Military assistance, proposals for reform, supplies, payment, and correspondence. British withdrawal and the limits of the intervention. How contemporary participants and later historians interpreted its significance.

51. **Kamaleshwar Singha and Partial Reconstruction** (1795–1811; working chronology): Suklingphaa/Kamaleshwar Singha, court, household, and ministerial authority. Purnananda’s administration, military recovery, settlement, and restoration of production. Matak autonomy, leadership, settlements, and the politics of territorial compromise. Continuing unrest, changing labour and military arrangements, and relations with neighbours. The limits of recovery and the succession to Chandrakanta Singha.

52. **Chandrakanta Singha and the Court’s Breakdown** (1811–1817; working chronology): Sudingphaa/Chandrakanta Singha: accession, household, factional politics, and competing officials. Purnananda, Badan Chandra Borphukan, Ruchinath, and other principal actors. Disputes over power, flight, appeals abroad, and the sequence leading to intervention. Contacts with the Company, Burmese court, and other regional actors. Distinguish documented decisions from later allegations of personal treachery or inevitability.

53. **The First Burmese Intervention** (1817): Burmese/Konbaung interests and the political circumstances of the invitation. Commanders, routes, battles, treaties or settlements, payments, and withdrawal. Badan Chandra’s role and changes in court power. Effects on the king, ministers, military organisation, inhabitants, and neighbouring polities. Compare Assamese, Burmese, and Company records and reconcile different invasion counts.

54. **Purandar Singha and the Second Intervention** (1818–1821; working chronology): Brajanath, Purandar Singha, Ruchinath, and the displacement of Chandrakanta. Purandar’s accession, administration, opposition, and short first reign. The renewed Burmese intervention, campaigns, flight, and Chandrakanta’s restoration. Shifting alliances, attempts to regain authority, payments, and military resources. Keep Purandar’s later 1833–1838 restoration in the contextual appendix rather than mixing the two reigns.

55. **Chandrakanta’s Resistance and Jogeshwar Singha** (1821–1824/1825; dates to reconcile): Chandrakanta’s attempts to recover control, recruitment, allies, and recorded battles. Jogeshwar Singha: lineage, installation, nominal status, household, and variant reign dates. Burmese commanders and the actual distribution of power. Flight, exile, rival royal claims, and the fragmentation of sovereignty. Resolve differences in accounts of the final campaigns and the occupation’s end.

56. **The Burmese Occupation and Civilian Catastrophe** (Approximately 1817–1826, with phases separated): Administration, extraction, garrisons, local collaborators, and changing forms of occupation. Violence, captivity, flight, famine, depopulation, and loss of cultivation and settlements. Experiences of women, children, religious institutions, and frontier communities. The Manor Din memory tradition: compare contemporary reports with later oral and literary accounts. Use ranges or qualitative descriptions where death tolls and population estimates cannot be established.

57. **The First Anglo-Burmese War and Yandabo** (1824–1826): Company and Konbaung objectives and the war’s wider geography. The Assam campaign, commanders, routes, battles, evacuation, and the end of Burmese control. The Treaty of Yandabo: identify the signatories and analyse the Assam-related wording. Distinguish Burma’s renunciation of claims from the subsequent means by which the Company governed Assam. Responses of Ahom claimants, nobles, local communities, and neighbouring polities.


## Part VII — Company rule and Gomdhar Konwar

58. **Company Rule and the Question of Restoration** (1826–1828): David Scott and Company officials, territorial divisions, revenue, military control, and local intermediaries. What proclamations and correspondence promised, and what decisions actually followed. Royal claimants, displaced nobles, service holders, cultivators, and grievances. Debates about restoring an Ahom ruler and the political status of Upper Assam. Networks and circumstances that made a royal restoration movement possible.

59. **Gomdhar Konwar: Biography, Claim, and Coronation** (Life dossier; principal focus 1828): Name variants, family, royal descent, birthplace, upbringing, and evidence for each biographical claim. Dhanjoy/Dhananjay Borgohain, Jairam/Jeuram Khargharia Phukan, and other supporters: verify identities and roles. The assembly, coronation or installation, location, dates, rituals, and declared claim to authority. Support networks, recruitment, resources, objectives, and relations with local communities. Treat Gomdhar as a restoration claimant or rebel king as sources warrant; do not silently add him to the ordinary pre-occupation regnal sequence.

60. **The Uprising of 1828 and Gomdhar’s Fate** (1828, plus a bounded biographical aftermath): Plans, movement of supporters, attempted operations, Company intelligence, and suppression. Neufville and other officials; arrest, surrender, proceedings, charges, and sentencing. Conflicting accounts of punishment, commutation, imprisonment, exile, release, and later life; trace each to its earliest available record. Why the movement failed, what it sought, and how historians interpret restoration and anti-colonial resistance. Close the narrative on Gomdhar; place the separate 1830 uprising and 1833–1838 restoration in a short clearly out-of-period appendix.
