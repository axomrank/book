### Scope and promise to the reader

The objective is a comprehensive, source-documented history of the Ahom dynasty, its kingdom, the people who lived within and beside it, its breakdown, and Gomdhar Konwar’s restoration attempt. Cover every ruler and interregnum, all recoverable significant political events, institutional change, society, economy, religion, languages, culture, monuments, warfare, diplomacy, and everyday life.

Literal inclusion of every internet statement is neither demonstrable nor desirable: websites duplicate one another, sources disagree, and some claims have no evidence. The working completeness standard is **every relevant, distinct, traceable claim found through a documented research process**, with a clear destination in the narrative, an appendix, or a register of disputes and unsupported claims. The work should synthesise evidence in original prose rather than copy online texts.

Gomdhar’s uprising in 1828 is the narrative endpoint. Necessary pre-1228 context is introductory. Later evidence about his personal fate may appear in a bounded biographical coda. The 1830 resistance, Purandar’s later restoration in 1833–1838, and modern heritage appear only in explicitly labelled contextual appendices or reception notes. The endpoint is a deliberate editorial boundary, not a claim that every later Ahom royal history ceased in 1828.

### Recommended scale and reader

- Format: one coherent reference work, preferably published in three volumes.
- Readers: serious general readers, students, researchers, and readers of Assamese and Tai-Ahom heritage.
- Language assumption: English drafting with Assamese names and selected Tai-Ahom forms; an Assamese edition can share the same research database.
- Main text: approximately 220,000–300,000 words, with a further 30,000–50,000 words of notes, catalogues, and appendices. This is an editorial target, not a measured requirement.
- Working print target: approximately 1,100 pages across three volumes; use 1,000–1,200 pages as the planning range. Pagination remains an estimate until the manuscript and illustrations are laid out.
- Recommended page size: 7 × 10 inches (177.8 × 254 mm), portrait. Aspect ratio: 7:10, width to height. These are design recommendations, not fixed production requirements.
- Volume lengths should follow the evidence and chapter requirements rather than be forced to match. Allow space for maps, genealogies, notes, appendices, bibliography, and indexes.
- Volume I: Chapters 1–16, origins, foundation, and expansion to 1603.
- Volume II: Chapters 17–34, war, succession, restoration, cultural patronage, and regional relations to 1769.
- Volume III: Chapters 35–60, the state and society, civil war, occupation, Company rule, and Gomdhar Konwar.
- A separate digital research catalogue should hold full event, person, place, source, object, and uncertainty records beyond what fits in print.

### Rules for chapter content

Political chapters use the same sequence: accession and context; principal actors; dated event narrative; administrative and social consequences; contested evidence; transition to the next period. Short reigns still receive individual subsections and a reign dossier. A long reign is not represented only by its most famous battle.

Thematic chapters explain change by period, place, community, and social position. They must not project an eighteenth-century institution unchanged back to the thirteenth century. Court-centred evidence is supplemented with archaeology, material culture, religious texts, and community perspectives where available.

Every substantial chapter should contain an opening question, a map or document when useful, clearly sourced quotations within appropriate limits, endnotes, an annotated further-reading list, a brief chronology, and an explicit explanation of unresolved points. Proposed visuals below are commissions for research and design, not existing verified illustrations.

### Reusable chapter development template

For every chapter, create a separate working manuscript with the following fields and structure:

1. **Identity:** chapter number, title, coverage dates, volume, draft version, and status.
2. **Purpose:** the question the chapter answers and its relationship to the chapters before and after it.
3. **Research brief:** the five numbered content subsections from this master plan, key actors and places, and the records needed to verify them.
4. **Evidence dossier:** sources actually consulted, exact page/folio or object references, chronological disagreements, translation issues, and unresolved claims.
5. **Opening:** a documented episode, object, place, or historical question that introduces the chapter. Do not invent conversations or private thoughts.
6. **Main narrative:** develop each planned subsection with context, evidence, chronology, interpretation, and consequences. Add smaller subheadings where necessary.
7. **People and wider effects:** connect political events to institutions, communities, women, households, labour, or the environment where the evidence permits.
8. **Supporting material:** maps, chronology, genealogies, document selections, diagrams, images, captions, credits, and cross-references.
9. **Disputed evidence:** explain competing accounts and why a conclusion is adopted, qualified, or left unresolved.
10. **Closing transition:** establish the outcome and the problem inherited by the next chapter without repeating a generic summary.
11. **Notes and further reading:** complete citations and an annotated reading list; record sources cited indirectly as such.
12. **Review record:** factual checks, specialist questions, missing permissions, editorial issues, and publication readiness.

Start with a working allocation of roughly 3,000–5,000 words per chapter, then adjust to the surviving evidence and importance of the subject. Major wars, institutional subjects, and long reigns may need more. Sparse early reigns should not be padded to meet a quota. Maintain the overall main-text target rather than demand identical chapter lengths.

## Required appendices and reference sections

A. **Complete regnal chronology.** Record every ruler, each separate reign of a returning ruler, interregna, competing claimants, Tai and Assamese names, titles, lineage, accession, death or removal, principal capital, main evidence, and alternative dates. Include early and short-lived rulers; distinguish rebel rulers and rulers installed under occupation from a regular succession table. A boxed note explains the later 1833–1838 restoration without extending the main narrative.

B. **Master event chronology, 1228–1828.** Include major and minor attested campaigns, accessions, diplomatic missions, grants, building works, rebellions, natural disasters, and institutional developments. If nothing reliable survives for a year, say so; do not fill annual gaps with imagined activity.

C. **Royal genealogy and important family trees.** Include branches, marriage alliances, succession disputes, the Tungkhungia line, royal women, and Gomdhar’s claimed descent with uncertainty marked.

D. **Biographical dictionary.** Kings, queens, princes, ministers, commanders, envoys, religious leaders, chroniclers, craftspeople when identifiable, rebel leaders, and foreign officials. Cross-reference people with similar names and avoid conflating Phukan, Barua, Gohain, and royal titles with unique personal names.

E. **Battle and campaign register.** Date, alternate name, location confidence, parties, commanders, objectives, campaign context, evidence, result, consequences, and disputed strength or casualties. Minor engagements need entries even when they do not justify a chapter.

F. **Treaties and diplomatic documents.** Short translated selections or summaries with the original-language source, date, signatories or parties, obligations, translation notes, and legal/political interpretation. Ghilajharighat, Asurar Ali, Yandabo, and other attested agreements belong here.

G. **Office, title, and institutional glossary.** Office functions, jurisdiction, eligibility, changes through time, variant spellings, and examples. Do not define an office only by its last known form.

H. **Monument and archaeological-site gazetteer.** Historical and present names, location, patron, construction phases, inscriptions, material, function, damage, surviving remains, source, and image permissions.

I. **Coin, inscription, seal, and object catalogue.** Provenance, collection, accession number where available, material, measurements when documented, inscription/transcription, translation, proposed date, attribution, and bibliography. No imagined objects or invented museum identifiers.

J. **Buranji and manuscript catalogue.** Original title, alternative title, author/editor, language/script, date of composition versus date of surviving copy, repository, shelfmark, edition, translation, coverage, and relationship to other witnesses.

K. **Historical geography and place-name concordance.** Rivers, capitals, forts, routes, markets, provinces, frontier zones, historic names, modern names, and approximate coordinates only where confidently identified. Modern district boundaries are reference layers rather than historical borders.

L. **Community, religious institution, and khel directory.** A dated cross-reference to communities, sattras, temples, specialist groups, offices, and service categories. Historical labels are explained without treating present identities as static through six centuries.

M. **Calendar and measurement guide.** Saka, Gregorian, and other source dating systems; local land and weight units. Keep original dates and units alongside any conversion and state the method and uncertainty.

N. **Disputed claims and popular stories.** Famous speeches; casualty and population totals; Lachit’s relative anecdote; Joymoti traditions; Mula Gabharu narratives; palace tunnels; construction-material folklore; chronicle destruction; and Gomdhar’s sentence, imprisonment, exile, and death. Each gets a source trail and an assessment rather than automatic acceptance or dismissal.

O. **Bounded aftermath, 1829–1838.** A short contextual bridge distinguishing Gomdhar’s movement from the later 1830 conspiracy/uprising and Purandar’s second reign. It is optional supplementary context, clearly outside the requested main period.

P. **Reception and heritage.** A short labelled modern section on literature, commemorations, preservation, and Tai-Ahom heritage. Modern recognition is evidence of later memory, not proof of a medieval event.

Q. **Bibliography, name index, place index, subject index, and image credits.** Index variant spellings and vernacular forms. Use an annotated bibliography to distinguish primary witnesses, translated editions, scholarship, textbooks, public-history pages, and leads not yet checked.

### Source hierarchy and claim handling

- Begin with contemporary and near-contemporary records and dated material evidence. Their proximity does not make them unbiased or automatically correct.
- Compare edited primary texts and translations with the underlying witness where possible; label later chronicles as later.
- Use scholarly monographs and peer-reviewed studies for interpretation, chronology, and source criticism.
- Government pages, university teaching notes, heritage summaries, journalism, Wikipedia, blogs, videos, and social media can reveal leads, memory, or popular claims. Trace substantial claims back to independent evidence.
- Multiple websites repeating one book are one source lineage, not multiple independent confirmations.
- Oral traditions and living community knowledge receive attribution, collection date, context, and an explanation of what they establish. They should neither be presented as contemporary documents nor discarded merely for being oral.
- Use source-appropriate wording: established, well-supported, plausible, disputed, traditional, or unverified. A confidence label cannot replace an explanatory note.

## Research system for comprehensive coverage

### 1. Create an authority file

Assign identifiers for people, places, dynastic branches, offices, events, institutions, objects, and manuscripts. Preserve spelling variants in English, Assamese, Tai-Ahom, Persian transliteration, and Burmese transliteration where applicable. Distinguish people who share a title or similar name.

### 2. Build source and claim registers

For every source record: identifier, author, title, language, source type, composition date, copy/edition date, translator/editor, repository, shelfmark or URL, access date, pages/folios consulted, cited predecessors, availability, and rights status.

For every substantive claim record: identifier, precise claim, date or range, place, people, theme, earliest located source, supporting source lineage, conflicting evidence, original-language wording or brief extract, page/folio, confidence and rationale, assigned chapter/appendix, and verification status.

The reader should be able to trace a claim about a battle, queen, treaty, land grant, or sentence to a specific passage or object rather than a generic bibliography entry.

### 3. Search systematically

Search English, Assamese, and variant romanisations. Work through each reign, each major officer, each queen, each region, and each thematic subject. Search publication bibliographies and follow citations backwards and forwards. Use old place names, alternate titles, and historical spelling variants.

Maintain a search log with database/site, query, language, date, result relevance, and sources followed. Search for negative and conflicting evidence as well as support. A missing digital result is not evidence that a historical event did not occur.

### 4. Maintain a coverage matrix

Rows: every reign and interregnum; each century/decade as appropriate; each principal region; every major community represented in the evidence; and each institutional or cultural theme.

Columns: politics, diplomacy, military history, administration, land/labour, trade/crafts, religion, women/family, language/literature, material culture, environment/daily life, relevant sources, disputes, assigned chapter, and research status.

Mark a field as researched, supported, unresolved, or insufficient evidence. Do not call an empty field complete merely because a famous king’s reign has already been drafted.

### 5. Assemble standard dossiers

- **Ruler dossier:** names/titles, lineage, household, accession, reign phases, ministers, campaigns, diplomacy, administration, patronage, social effects, death/removal, succession, conflicting dates, and evidence.
- **Event dossier:** chronology, location, protagonists, causes, sequence, contemporary descriptions, outcome, consequences, disputed claims, and maps.
- **Institution dossier:** earliest evidence, period-specific form, jurisdiction, personnel, resources, change, lived effects, and decline/transformation.
- **Person dossier:** name variants, relationships, career, actions, documentary record, later memory, and unknowns.
- **Site/object dossier:** provenance, location, attribution, dating, material, inscription, conservation, bibliography, and permissioned images.

### 6. Apply a realistic research completion rule

Before drafting is signed off, every ruler and interregnum has a dossier; every chapter topic has a documented search; substantive claims have page- or object-level citations; competing source traditions have been compared where available; no high-impact contradiction has been silently resolved; and the appendices account for significant details omitted from the narrative.

A defined second search pass should test for missed topics and new scholarship. Record the final research cutoff date. State that completeness is bounded by the defined source corpus and available evidence, not by a claim to have read the whole internet.
