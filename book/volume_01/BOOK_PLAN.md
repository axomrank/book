# Volume 1 — Origins, Foundation and Expansion

Series: **From Sukaphaa to Gomdhar Konwar: A History of the Ahom Dynasty**.

**Scope:** Origins and necessary pre-1228 background; foundation and expansion through 1603.

**Chapters:** 1–16.

These chapter briefs are extracted from the [series master plan](../Ahom_Dynasty_Book_Plan_1228_to_Gomdhar_Konwar.md). Shared evidence rules, production recommendations, appendices, and source strategy remain in that master. It is the authority if a volume extract becomes out of date; update both when revising a brief.

### Chapter 01. How We Know Ahom History

**Coverage:** Method and sources.

1.1. Buranjis in Tai-Ahom and Assamese: authorship, manuscript families, copying, translation, and court purposes.

1.2. Inscriptions, copper plates, coins, archaeology, diplomatic letters, religious literature, genealogies, and material culture.

1.3. Persian, Burmese, British, and other external accounts: what each can and cannot establish.

1.4. Colonial, nationalist, regional, community, and recent academic interpretations; missing voices and archival silences.

1.5. Rules for separating documented events, probable reconstructions, oral traditions, legends, and modern commemorative claims.

**Planned supporting material:** Source-family diagram and a sample comparison of conflicting accounts.

### Chapter 02. The Brahmaputra Valley on the Eve of 1228

**Coverage:** Background to 1228.

2.1. River systems, floodplains, forests, hill passages, wet-rice zones, and travel constraints.

2.2. Political landscape after the fragmentation of Kamarupa; distinguish later state boundaries from medieval ones.

2.3. Moran, Borahi, Chutiya, Kachari/Dimasa, Bhuyan, and other communities: their own histories and changing relationships.

2.4. Existing settlement, cultivation, exchange, religion, and languages before Ahom arrival.

2.5. How geography shaped the possibilities for state formation, warfare, and diplomacy.

**Planned supporting material:** A background map with approximate political zones and a river-and-route map.

### Chapter 03. Tai Origins and Royal Ancestry

**Coverage:** Before 1228; introductory background.

3.1. Tai-speaking worlds, Mong Mao and related regional polities, migration traditions, and historical geography.

3.2. Sukaphaa’s ancestry, titles, companions, and variant genealogies; document what is uncertain.

3.3. Khunlung–Khunlai and divine-origin narratives as traditions of legitimacy rather than dated political fact.

3.4. Language, ritual specialists, agricultural knowledge, and forms of leadership carried into Assam.

3.5. Relationship between Tai-Ahom identity, the royal dynasty, and the later multi-community kingdom.

**Planned supporting material:** An annotated genealogy and a map of the proposed homeland and routes.

### Chapter 04. Across the Patkai: Arrival in 1228

**Coverage:** Journey and arrival.

4.1. Competing accounts of departure, migration stages, route, companions, animals, and dates.

4.2. Patkai crossings and encounters with frontier communities, including both negotiation and violence where supported.

4.3. Arrival in the Brahmaputra valley and the conventional significance of 1228.

4.4. Settlement choices, river crossings, scouting, food supply, and adaptation.

4.5. Identify later heroic additions and unsupported numerical claims in popular accounts.

**Planned supporting material:** A route map marking secure, approximate, and disputed locations separately.

### Chapter 05. Sukaphaa and the Founding Kingdom

**Coverage:** 1228–1268; working chronology.

5.1. Settlement phases, the establishment of Charaideo, and the growth of the first court.

5.2. Moran and Borahi relations: alliances, intermarriage, conflict, incorporation, and local agency.

5.3. Early institutions, leading officers, land, rice cultivation, labour, and royal household.

5.4. The foundations of kingship, ritual life, succession, and historical record keeping.

5.5. Sukaphaa’s death, succession, funerary traditions, and the difference between contemporary evidence and later memory.

**Planned supporting material:** A settlement sequence and early-state institutional diagram.

### Chapter 06. The First Successors: Suteuphaa, Subinphaa, and Sukhaangphaa

**Coverage:** 1268–1332; working chronology.

6.1. Individual reign dossiers for Suteuphaa, Subinphaa, and Sukhaangphaa, including alternate spellings.

6.2. Early territorial consolidation and relations with neighbouring rulers and communities.

6.3. Royal lineages, clan traditions, nobles, and succession arrangements.

6.4. Cultivation, population incorporation, and development of settlements and offices.

6.5. Short notices and uncertain episodes retained in an appendix rather than inflated into invented narratives.

**Planned supporting material:** A succession chart and comparative reign table.

### Chapter 07. Succession Crises and Rule without a King

**Coverage:** 1332–1397; working chronology.

7.1. Sukhrangphaa, Sutuphaa, and Tyao Khamti: separate biographies and reign narratives.

7.2. Political roles of nobles, royal households, and competing claimants.

7.3. Interregna: who exercised authority, why a ruler was not installed, and what sources say.

7.4. Assassination traditions, external encounters, and chronology disagreements.

7.5. Institutional continuity despite disputed succession; avoid assuming the state ceased to exist during an interregnum.

**Planned supporting material:** A king-and-interregnum timeline with competing date columns.

### Chapter 08. Sudangphaa: The Bamuni Konwar

**Coverage:** 1397–1407; working chronology.

8.1. Childhood and upbringing traditions, recovery of royal identity, and accession.

8.2. Brahmanical influence, court ritual, royal legitimacy, and limits of claims about an abrupt religious conversion.

8.3. Noble opposition and the political consolidation of the reign.

8.4. Frontier diplomacy, campaigns, and agreements attributed to this ruler.

8.5. Death, succession, and the later significance of the Bamuni Konwar story.

**Planned supporting material:** An accession narrative with evidence labels and a frontier map.

### Chapter 09. The Fifteenth-Century Kingdom

**Coverage:** 1407–1497; working chronology.

9.1. Individual sections for Sujangphaa, Suphakphaa, Susenphaa, Suhenphaa, and Supimphaa.

9.2. Frontier relations, campaigns, internal conflict, and succession changes under each ruler.

9.3. Settlement, cultivation, administrative growth, and integration of communities.

9.4. Noble families, royal kinship, court violence, and competing historical explanations.

9.5. A complete catalogue of smaller recorded events and gaps in surviving documentation.

**Planned supporting material:** Five ruler dossiers and a map showing changes in the eastern kingdom.

### Chapter 10. Suhungmung and a Larger State

**Coverage:** 1497–1539; working chronology.

10.1. Accession, royal names and titles, household, court, and political ambitions.

10.2. Territorial growth as a series of campaigns and negotiations, not one uniform conquest.

10.3. Administrative innovations, regional offices, settlement, and incorporation of new populations.

10.4. Changes in royal language, titles, religious patronage, and political identity.

10.5. Opposition, succession struggle, death, and an assessment of the reign through multiple source traditions.

**Planned supporting material:** A before-and-after territorial map with dated layers.

### Chapter 11. The Chutiya Kingdom and Ahom Expansion

**Coverage:** Early sixteenth century.

11.1. Chutiya political history and institutions before incorporation; include Chutiya perspectives.

11.2. Campaign sequence, rulers, commanders, locations, and disputed dates.

11.3. Traditions concerning Sati Sadhani/Sadhini: distinguish documentary history, oral tradition, and commemoration.

11.4. Sadiya administration, population movements, craft knowledge, religious sites, and military resources.

11.5. Resistance, accommodation, and long-term changes after conquest.

**Planned supporting material:** A campaign map and a comparison of Ahom and Chutiya narratives.

### Chapter 12. Ahom–Kachari/Dimasa Relations

**Coverage:** Sixteenth century, with necessary background.

12.1. Kachari/Dimasa political geography and the significance of Dimapur and later centres.

12.2. Campaigns, commanders, diplomatic demands, settlements, and relocations.

12.3. Weapons, forts, river routes, and limits of battlefield reconstructions.

12.4. Consequences for trade, frontier control, population, and administration.

12.5. Distinguish the histories of neighbouring peoples from a narrative told only from the Ahom court.

**Planned supporting material:** A changing-frontier map and campaign-and-treaty table.

### Chapter 13. Bengal Campaigns, Turbak, and Mula Gabharu

**Coverage:** Early sixteenth century.

13.1. Bengal’s political context and the sequence of invasions and counter-campaigns.

13.2. Turbak, Ahom commanders, military adaptations, and the battles recorded in different accounts.

13.3. Mula Gabharu’s life, actions, source history, and later representation.

13.4. Captives, settlement, craft skills, religious communities, and the aftermath of warfare.

13.5. Check famous speeches, casualty totals, battle locations, and claims about the origins of institutions.

**Planned supporting material:** A campaign atlas page and a Mula Gabharu evidence dossier.

### Chapter 14. Suklenmung and Garhgaon

**Coverage:** 1539–1552; working chronology.

14.1. Accession, lineage, major officers, and the structure of the court.

14.2. The development of Garhgaon: landscape, defence, settlement, and royal space.

14.3. Campaigns, frontier consolidation, roads, and other works attributed to the reign.

14.4. Coins, inscriptions, titles, and religious patronage where surviving evidence permits.

14.5. Death, succession, and links between the expanded state and its next phase.

**Planned supporting material:** A capital map and catalogue of securely attributed material evidence.

### Chapter 15. Sukhaamphaa and the Koch Challenge

**Coverage:** 1552–1603; working chronology.

15.1. The long reign and the Khora Raja tradition; royal kinship and principal officers.

15.2. Nara Narayan, Chilarai, Koch power, and the sequence of Ahom–Koch confrontations.

15.3. Defeat, terms of settlement, hostages, tribute, and political recovery.

15.4. Territorial limits, frontier security, alliances, and the later division of Koch power.

15.5. Long-term consequences for relations with Bengal and the emerging Mughal frontier.

**Planned supporting material:** An Ahom–Koch comparison and a dated frontier sequence.

### Chapter 16. Diplomacy and the Regional Order before 1603

**Coverage:** Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries; synthesis.

16.1. Comparative relations with Kamata/Koch, Chutiya, Kachari/Dimasa, Bhuyans, and eastern Tai polities.

16.2. Envoys, marriage, gifts, tribute, hostage arrangements, written agreements, and ceremonial ranking.

16.3. Markets, passes, buffer zones, and the difference between tribute and direct administration.

16.4. Periodic conflict and negotiation with hill and frontier communities; terminology must be historically explained.

16.5. A source-backed register of every recoverable diplomatic mission before 1603.

**Planned supporting material:** A diplomatic-network map and mission register.
