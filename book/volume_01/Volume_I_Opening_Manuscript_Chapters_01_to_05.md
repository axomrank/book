# Chapter 1 — How We Know Ahom History

**Volume I: Origins, Foundation, and Expansion to 1603**  
**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; requires wider scholarly and manuscript comparison.

## 1.1 The kingdom in its own records

The history of the Ahom kingdom reaches us through records that were themselves part of its political life. A chronicle could preserve the order of rulers, describe a campaign, identify an officer, explain a marriage, or establish the standing of a family. These purposes mattered to the people who produced and kept the records. They also matter to the reader trying to reconstruct a world that began centuries before the surviving printed editions.

Buranjis form the principal narrative foundation of this history. Their survival gives the Ahom past an unusual depth of written remembrance, but a buranji is not a window through which events can be viewed without interference. Its wording belongs to a particular text, language, manuscript history, and political setting. An account of the thirteenth century copied much later is evidence for the historical tradition concerning that century. Its date of copying does not make the copyist an eyewitness to Sukaphaa’s arrival.

The route from manuscript to modern book can be followed in the work of Golap Chandra Barua. In the preface to his 1930 bilingual *Ahom-Buranji*, Barua describes a manuscript obtained in 1894 from a Deodhai pandit in the Sibsagar area. It was written on prepared bark and translated with the assistance of Deodhai scholars. Barua distinguishes a section concerned with divine origins from one concerned with earthly history. He also explains that the modern reign headings and paragraph divisions were editorial additions to a text originally organised differently.[^c1-b-preface]

This is a useful warning about the appearance of a printed source. A heading such as “Sukaphaa” may look like part of the original chronicle’s architecture when it was supplied to help later readers. An English rendering may clarify an unfamiliar office by placing a better-known title in brackets. Such help is valuable, but the clarification must not be confused with the manuscript’s exact wording. A history that depends on translation should make that dependence visible.

## 1.2 Evidence beyond the narrative

A kingdom also leaves traces that do not take the form of a continuous story. Inscriptions, coins, buildings, earthworks, burial places, religious records, and family genealogies answer different questions. A dated inscription may establish that a grant was issued by a particular ruler. It does not automatically confirm every episode assigned to that ruler in a chronicle. A surviving structure may demonstrate an architectural practice, while the identity of its builder remains open to examination.

Edward Gait’s 1906 *A History of Assam* describes a research programme initiated in the 1890s that collected manuscripts alongside inscriptions, copper plates, and coins. The discoveries included records of earlier dynasties and materials concerning neighbouring states. These were not all documents of Ahom rule, and their usefulness changes with the period under discussion. Evidence about the valley before 1228 cannot be treated as evidence about the institutions of the kingdom two hundred years later.[^c1-g-intro]

The royal funerary landscape at Charaideo supplies another kind of evidence. Its moidams make royal death, sacred geography, and the persistence of a dynastic centre subjects of material study. The UNESCO description identifies the property as the necropolis of the Tai-Ahom dynasty. That modern heritage account provides orientation to the site; dating an individual burial or identifying its occupant requires the relevant archaeological and historical documentation.[^c1-u]

The strongest reconstruction therefore begins with a question and asks which evidence can answer it. A chronicle is well suited to investigating how a court narrated succession. Archaeology can test a proposed construction sequence. A genealogy can show how a family represented its position, while leaving uncertainty about the antiquity of that representation. No category deserves universal priority irrespective of the claim being made.

## 1.3 Looking from outside

External accounts widen the field of view. They may identify a ruler under another name, supply a different date, describe a campaign from the opposing side, or expose interests omitted by a court narrative. Their value depends on proximity to the event and on the information available to their authors. Distance from the Ahom court does not itself guarantee independence or accuracy.

For this volume, accounts concerning eastern Tai polities, Bengal, Kamata and Koch rulers, and Kachari or Dimasa histories are especially important. They can help establish the regional setting of early Ahom expansion. Persian narratives become more abundant for later conflicts, but a seventeenth-century Mughal account cannot simply be used to fill a gap in a thirteenth-century migration story. The date and scope of each witness must remain attached to it.

Gait illustrates both the usefulness and the hazards of comparison. Discussing the early sixteenth-century Bengal campaigns, he notes difficulties in identifying commanders and reconciling accounts from different traditions. He also distinguishes alternative casualty totals rather than treating the larger number as necessarily correct.[^c1-g-war] These discrepancies are part of the historical evidence. They tell us which details need further work and where confidence should stop.

An apparent agreement between two books can also conceal dependence. Gait did not read Tai-Ahom himself; he relied on translations prepared through Barua and Deodhai scholars. Barua subsequently acknowledged that the chronicle he published had been used for Gait’s history.[^c1-lineage] Their agreement is useful for checking how a tradition was rendered and summarised. It is not automatically two independent confirmations of an event.

## 1.4 The historians and the silences

Every history chooses a scale of attention. Royal chronicles commonly make rulers, ministers, battles, and elite marriages more visible than cultivators, craft workers, servants, or the everyday activities of women. The absence of a person from a court record does not establish the absence of that person’s contribution. It establishes a limit in what that record chose or managed to preserve.

Barua was unusually direct about omissions. His preface says that the chronicle left important people and subjects inadequately covered, including religious figures and the founding of religious institutions. He points towards other genres of evidence, including genealogical records, to supplement it.[^c1-b-preface] A comprehensive history must therefore follow more than one documentary path. Even a detailed buranji cannot be made to carry the entire social history of a kingdom.

Gait’s book remains a substantial guide to documents and chronology. Its explanations must nevertheless be examined separately from the evidence it assembled. His introduction makes sweeping claims about the supposed absence of historical understanding elsewhere in India, and his discussion of peoples uses racial and environmental assumptions characteristic of colonial scholarship.[^c1-g-bias] Those assumptions are not adopted here. Political ability, agricultural knowledge, and cultural change cannot be explained by ranking communities according to inherited racial qualities.

Later interpretations also require examination. A nationalist narrative may place resistance at its centre; a dynastic narrative may privilege continuity; a community history may restore experiences obscured by the victorious court. Each can raise important questions. None removes the need to trace a claim to evidence or to distinguish a remembered meaning from an independently dated event.

## 1.5 A reader’s guide to certainty

This book uses different kinds of statements deliberately. “The chronicle records” identifies a report in a particular source. “The evidence suggests” marks an interpretation. A traditional origin story is presented as a tradition, not inserted into a verified list of political events. A date used to organise the narrative may remain a working date while conflicting readings are assessed in a note.

The distinction is especially important for the period before and immediately after 1228. Genealogies linking rulers to divine ancestors speak to legitimacy and sacred order. They cannot be tested in the same manner as a dated record of a diplomatic mission. Numerical claims about a migrating population require more than repetition in modern summaries. A precise figure can be a transmitted feature of a story without being a measured count.

Nor does uncertainty require abandoning the narrative. Sukaphaa’s arrival, the development of a court, the succession of rulers, and the expansion of the kingdom can be narrated while the reader is shown where the sources differ. The aim is to make an intelligible history without concealing the steps by which it has been reconstructed.

The opening task is therefore to establish the world into which Sukaphaa came. That world was already inhabited, cultivated, governed, and remembered. The dynasty’s history begins within it.

# Chapter 2 — The Brahmaputra Valley on the Eve of 1228

**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; political zones remain approximate.

## 2.1 Rivers and the choice of a home

The settlement history attributed to Sukaphaa makes geography an active part of the foundation story. Rivers carry his followers towards possible homes. Flooding helps explain why they leave them. A place that permits arrival does not necessarily permit a durable court. The early narrative is a sequence of adjustments to water, food, neighbours, and the practical demands of staying.

Barua’s translated chronicle describes movement along the Dihing, residence at Tipam, a stay at Abhaypur, and a subsequent move to Habung. Floodwater recurs in the explanation of these changes. The account also represents the inhabitants and their harvests as matters important to the migrating group. Later, the court is established beside a hill at Charaideo.[^c2-b-route] These passages do not provide a modern environmental survey. They preserve a historical tradition in which the success of settlement depends on understanding a landscape.

The valley should accordingly be imagined as more than an expanse of cultivable ground. A river can connect settlements and separate them. A crossing can become a point of negotiation. A flooded tract can be temporarily unusable while another nearby site remains productive. Movement between hill passages, tributaries, and the main river links different environments and different bodies of local knowledge.

The chronicle’s account does not establish the exact medieval course of every river. Modern place names and channels cannot simply be placed on a map and labelled “1228”. Where identification remains uncertain, the map accompanying this history will mark an approximate association or leave a location unresolved. The reader should be able to distinguish the route transmitted by a source from the geographical reconstruction made by an editor.

## 2.2 A political landscape with several centres

Sukaphaa did not enter a region lacking political history. Earlier kingdoms, local chiefs, religious establishments, and cultivated settlements formed the background to the new dynasty. Nor was the whole valley governed by a single authority at the moment of arrival. A history of Ahom expansion must begin with the plurality of that setting.

Gait’s account describes Chutiya power towards the east and Kachari power across substantial tracts south of the Brahmaputra, with Bhuyan chiefs in other areas. It places Moran and Borahi communities within the region of the earliest Ahom settlements.[^c2-g-zones] This supplies an initial orientation, but its schematic arrangement should not be turned into an exact boundary map for one particular year. A later chronicle can preserve the names of neighbouring powers without demonstrating precisely where their authority ended in 1228.

Kamarupa and Kamata also require careful handling. These names belong to changing political and geographical usages. They do not denote two timeless areas whose boundaries can be carried unchanged through the whole medieval period. Gait’s discussion itself shows the difficulty of relating a title, a place name, and the territory of a ruler.[^c2-g-kamata] The chapters that follow will attach each diplomatic or military encounter to its own period, rather than treat every mention of a western ruler as evidence for the same enduring state.

This approach changes the meaning of “expansion”. The extension of Ahom influence might take the form of a defeated ruler, a marriage, a tribute arrangement, the installation of an officer, or the movement of a population. These are different relationships. A campaign that reached a place does not by itself establish that the court thereafter administered it continuously.

## 2.3 Communities with histories of their own

Moran, Borahi, Chutiya, Kachari or Dimasa, and Bhuyan histories cannot be reduced to the roles assigned to them in the rise of the Ahom court. The court’s records are indispensable, but their field of vision is political and selective. When they describe another people as conquered or incorporated, they report a relationship from one side of it.

The names themselves need explanation. A designation used by a medieval or later chronicler may cover a ruler, a cluster of settlements, a political following, or a community. It need not coincide exactly with the meaning attached to the same name today. Historical discussion should preserve the name in its source while explaining the uncertainty of the correspondence.

Gait’s treatment of the Bhuyans offers a particular caution. He describes variation in the lists and traditions attached to the celebrated group of twelve chiefs.[^c2-g-bhuyan] The number should therefore not be used to invent a permanent council of exactly twelve members governing a fixed territory throughout the period. Individual families, claims to rank, and changing combinations of authority require separate investigation.

The treatment of Chutiya origins raises a related problem. Gait records traditions of ancestry and early rulers, but the presence of a genealogy does not make its entire sequence independently dated history.[^c2-g-chutiya] These traditions deserve a place because they show how a political past was remembered. They also require comparison with Chutiya perspectives and with material or documentary evidence that is independent of the Ahom conquest narrative.

Recovering local agency means following the relationships described in the sources closely. Who remained in place? Who supplied labour or food? Which chiefs negotiated? Which families entered the court? Where these questions cannot yet be answered, the gap should remain visible. A smooth sentence about the incorporation of a whole people can conceal many different experiences.

## 2.4 Cultivation, exchange, and existing knowledge

Food production is fundamental to the settlement story, but the relationship between migration and agricultural change is contested. It is easy to make a dramatic claim that the Ahoms introduced cultivation to an empty or unproductive valley. The sources consulted for this draft do not justify such a claim.

The translated buranji describes Sukaphaa’s followers living through successive harvests at Habung.[^c2-b-route] Whatever the exact historical circumstances behind that notice, the narrative itself does not describe a land without food production. To turn it into such a landscape would require overlooking the people already present in the story.

Modern discussion also cautions against a simple account of a single agricultural beginning. Hemanta Kumar Saud’s 2018 thesis notes uncertainty about the relative importance of wet and dry rice, and of permanent and shifting cultivation, in the pre-Ahom valley.[^c2-saud] That observation does not settle which community introduced which technique in a particular locality. It makes the question more precise: crop, method, area, period, and evidence must be identified separately.

Rice cultivation, water management, ironworking, livestock, craft production, and exchange should therefore be investigated as practices with histories. A new ruling group may bring knowledge, organise its use on a different scale, or learn from existing residents. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. The account of agricultural change must be built from evidence, rather than from an assumption that political arrival necessarily marks technological invention.

Religion and language deserve the same attention to coexistence. The beginnings of a dynasty do not erase the practices of the people around it. The source base presently assembled is strongest on elite relations and movement. A fuller account of the valley’s everyday life before 1228 needs inscriptions, archaeology, historical linguistics, and research outside the dynastic narrative.

## 2.5 Geography and the possibility of a kingdom

The early settlement tradition suggests that founding a kingdom required several problems to be solved together. A court needed a location. Its following needed food. Its leaders needed working relations with neighbours. Routes had to connect settlements without exposing movement entirely to flooding or resistance. These are analytical conclusions drawn from the narrative, not a list of policies recorded in Sukaphaa’s own words.

The repeated moves are significant because they give foundation a duration. Arrival in 1228 and the establishment of Charaideo in the conventional year 1253 are different moments. Between them lies a remembered process of searching, settling, leaving, and settling again.[^c2-g-settlement] The story is less intelligible if that process is compressed into the instant of a border crossing.

The possibilities were also unequal. People who knew the land possessed information indispensable to newcomers. Migrants who arrived with a leader and a following possessed their own means of coordination. The kingdom developed through encounters between such resources, including conflict. Geography shaped the conditions under which these encounters occurred; it did not dictate a single inevitable result.

The next chapter follows the traditions carried into this landscape. Before examining the crossing of the Patkai, it asks what the surviving sources mean when they connect the dynasty to a wider Tai world and to royal ancestors beyond the valley.

# Chapter 3 — Tai Origins and Royal Ancestry

**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; genealogy and homeland identification remain provisional.

## 3.1 A wider world to the east

The beginnings of Ahom history extend beyond the Brahmaputra valley. Its royal traditions connect Sukaphaa to a wider world of Tai rulers, related lineages, and places reached through routes east of the Patkai. Those connections are essential to understanding the dynasty. They must be approached without turning the boundaries and national identities of the present into the boundaries and identities of the thirteenth century.

Gait located the relevant Tai or Shan political world across parts of what are now Myanmar and Yunnan. He discussed names such as Mung Mao, Pong, and Maulung while acknowledging problems in identifying places and reconciling accounts.[^c3-g-regional] His geographical discussion is a starting point, not a warrant to place every transmitted name at an exact modern coordinate. A name may have been rendered differently in different languages or applied to more than one political centre.

The term Tai is most useful here as an orientation to linguistic and cultural relationships. It does not imply a single unified medieval people under one government. Nor does it make every Tai polity a stage in the history of the Ahom dynasty. The task is to identify the particular connections preserved in Ahom sources and compare them with evidence from the regions concerned.

This means distinguishing three scales: the wider Tai-speaking world, the lineage and following associated with Sukaphaa, and the kingdom that developed in Assam. Their histories overlap, but they are not interchangeable. The expanding kingdom eventually included people whose ancestry and languages did not derive from the migrating royal group.

## 3.2 The ancestry of a founder

Genealogy makes a ruler intelligible within a line of succession. It connects a person to predecessors, explains a claim to rank, and distinguishes legitimate authority from rival claims. For the founding dynasty, ancestry also links the settlement in Assam to a political world beyond it.

Barua’s edition preserves more than one passage bearing on Sukaphaa’s ancestry and departure. In its miscellaneous accounts, a sequence of ancestors culminates in a ruler whose sons are associated with neighbouring countries and with Sukaphaa. The text then explains Sukaphaa’s departure towards the west. Elsewhere, in the account of his migration, the narrative describes consultation with his grandfather and a country left to the rule of a brother.[^c3-b-ancestry]

These passages should be compared before a single family tree is printed. Their relationship is not settled merely by smoothing the different names into one spelling. It is necessary to ask whether the text is offering parallel traditions, a compressed genealogy, or a sequence whose titles and relationships have been altered in translation or copying. Until that work is done, a diagram should retain the distinctions between witnesses.

The edition also names companions, families, and ancestors of ritual specialists. These lists help explain how later families understood their connection to the foundation. They do not necessarily establish a complete passenger list for one departure. A founding genealogy can combine a memory of migration with an account of privileges and offices meaningful to descendants.

For this reason, the present narrative uses Sukaphaa as its preferred working spelling while retaining Shukapha and other source forms in the name register. A consistent spelling helps readers recognise the ruler across chapters; it must not hide a disputed identity or resolve a disagreement that goes beyond spelling.

## 3.3 Khunlung and Khunlai

The tradition of Khunlung and Khunlai takes the dynasty’s origin beyond ordinary political succession. In Barua’s translation, they are sent from heaven to earth by the lord of thunder and arrive with companions and families. Their descent is connected with the beginnings of earthly rule.[^c3-b-divine] Gait supplies a fuller summary involving divine ancestry, a descending chain, sacred objects, and instructions for royal conduct.[^c3-g-divine]

The historical question is not whether this can be entered into a modern calendar as an observed event. It is what the tradition says about legitimate kingship. Authority comes with ancestry, obligations, ritual objects, and a relationship between the earthly court and a larger sacred order. The narrative situates the ruler among more than human predecessors.

The same tradition cannot be made into an unqualified list of medieval kings merely by attaching a date to its beginning. Gait reports a traditional date of 568 for the descent story.[^c3-g-divine] This book records that date as part of the transmitted tradition. It does not use it as the starting point of a verified political chronology.

Different explanatory traditions also need to remain distinct. Gait describes an alternative connection with Indra and Vasistha. Such a version should not be treated as interchangeable with every Tai cosmological term.[^c3-g-alt] An English translator’s identification of a deity through a familiar Hindu name may express an interpretation. Understanding that interpretation requires attention to the original language and to the setting in which the correspondence was made.

Origin narratives can therefore be read seriously without being literalised. They are evidence for remembered legitimacy, sacred relationships, and the language of rule. Their meaning does not depend on pretending that a historian has independently verified a celestial descent.

## 3.4 What travelled with the following

Migration carries more than people. The chronicles represent Sukaphaa as bringing leaders, animals, sacred objects, and families connected with particular functions. Barua’s miscellaneous accounts associate companions with the ancestry of Deodhai and Mohan ritual specialists. The migration account names an object rendered as Sheng or Shengden; another passage uses a form glossed through Somdeo or Sengdeo.[^c3-b-carried]

These details make ritual continuity a visible part of the tradition. The founder’s following is represented as a community capable of sustaining leadership and ceremonial life. The sacred object is not merely luggage. Its presence binds the journey to the legitimacy that the dynasty claimed after settling.

The accounts also contain officers and ranks. Here translation deserves particular care. Barua sometimes explains a Tai designation through an Assamese office name in parentheses. The equivalence may help a reader of the edition, while leaving open whether the functions were identical across periods. It would be unsafe to turn every such gloss into proof that the entire later administrative structure already existed at departure.

Agricultural knowledge is another important research question, but it cannot be deduced simply from linguistic ancestry. The evidence for cultivation must be assessed by method and locality. The traditions establish that the following sought and occupied productive settlements; the full history of techniques and their transmission requires comparison beyond royal migration narratives.

Nor can the existence of surviving written traditions establish that the physical manuscripts now available were produced in the thirteenth century. The dynasty’s association with record keeping and the dates of surviving copies are separate questions. A future examination of script, manuscript material, and textual relationships must connect them carefully.

## 3.5 Identity after arrival

The dynasty retained an account of its origins while developing new relationships within the valley. The two processes can coexist. A ruling house may preserve a genealogy reaching eastward while its household, officers, subjects, and language practices change through local alliances and the incorporation of other populations.

Gait describes relations with Morans and Borahis in the founding period, including conflict and intermarriage.[^c3-g-local] Those relations are part of the history of the kingdom’s formation. They should not be described as the instant disappearance of distinct communities. Nor should the names of the dynasty and kingdom be assumed to identify everyone living under the court’s influence.

Tai-Ahom identity, royal descent, and membership in the kingdom are therefore treated separately throughout this volume. The distinction allows the reader to follow both continuity and change: continuity in rituals, lineage claims, and remembered origins; change in the people and institutions through which authority was exercised.

Sukaphaa’s journey belongs to this meeting of histories. The sources preserve a movement out of an eastern political world and into a landscape where other powers and communities already existed. The next chapter examines that movement as closely as the present evidence permits, including its conflict, its uncertain numbers, and the long process that separates arrival from a durable seat of rule.

# Chapter 4 — Across the Patkai: Arrival in 1228

**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; route, calendar conversions, and population figures require further checks.

## 4.1 A migration preserved in several forms

The conventional beginning of Ahom rule in Assam is 1228. That date gives the reader a clear point of orientation. The story attached to it is more complicated than the arrival of a ruler at a border. It includes departure, movement through several territories, encounters with local powers, river travel, and the search for places where the following could remain.

Gait’s account places Sukaphaa’s departure in 1215 and his arrival in the Khamjang region in 1228, after a journey of thirteen years. It gives a following of nine thousand people, eight nobles, two elephants, and three hundred horses.[^c4-g-numbers] These are figures reported by a historian working from chronicle translations. They are not an independently verified census of migrants.

Barua’s edition allows a closer view of one numerical feature. The migration passage describes three thousand cooking pots, each said to hold food for three men, alongside named elephants and three hundred horses.[^c4-b-pots] The arithmetic yields nine thousand portions. It does not by itself settle the number of people actually present, whether all were counted in the same way, or the age and sex composition of the following. Gait’s further suggestion about the proportion of adult men must remain his inference.

Precision in a narrative can be memorable and politically meaningful. It does not remove the need to investigate how a figure was transmitted. The final book should preserve these numbers as source reports while resisting the temptation to calculate a modern demographic profile from them.

## 4.2 The frontier was inhabited

The passage towards the valley took Sukaphaa through territories with their own inhabitants and chiefs. Barua’s translation names stages and associates some of them with resistance, submission, or the placement of a representative. Its account does not describe an empty corridor waiting for a new ruler.[^c4-b-frontier]

The text also contains severe violence attributed to Sukaphaa’s forces, including destruction of villages, captivity, and an atrocity involving coerced consumption of human flesh. These reports should neither be removed to protect a heroic image nor presented as independently established detail without qualification.[^c4-b-violence] They belong to the chronicle’s representation of conquest and fear. Comparison with other witnesses and a review of the translation are needed before the event is reconstructed more closely.

The historical term Naga, as used in these accounts, also needs care. It must not be treated as a precise identification of every present-day community in the region. A source may use a broad designation for different settlements or peoples. Its language tells us something about the chronicler’s categories; it does not give permission to generalise the character or conduct of their descendants.

Barua’s miscellaneous account presents another aspect of the movement. It describes people offering tribute, being permitted to remain, and some joining the following.[^c4-b-tribute] These statements cannot automatically be assigned to every named stage of the more detailed route. They nevertheless show that the transmitted story contains several forms of encounter: fighting, submission, continued local residence, and recruitment.

Migration and conquest are therefore not entirely separate categories in the source narrative. A moving royal following could seek passage, provisions, recognition, and authority at the same time. What happened at a particular place must be examined on its own evidence.

## 4.3 What 1228 can establish

The date 1228 anchors the conventional regnal chronology. It should not be made to prove that a mature kingdom, with its later territorial reach and administrative offices, came into existence that year. Arrival and durable institutional development are different historical questions.

Gait regarded the chronology from Sukaphaa’s entry onwards as much firmer than the divine and ancestral narratives preceding it.[^c4-g-boundary] That judgement helped organise subsequent histories. It is still necessary to check the events attached to individual years. A chronological framework can be useful even where details within it remain uncertain.

Calendar conversion is one source of difficulty. Barua’s translated passages retain named cyclical years and occasionally give an equivalent Christian-era date. The migration account does not convert every stage into a year that a modern reader can immediately compare with Gait’s sequence.[^c4-b-years] Conversion must therefore be examined as part of the research, rather than supplied casually from an unreferenced online timeline.

This book uses 1228 as a working beginning for the dynasty’s rule in Assam. It treats the route towards it as a transmitted and partly reconstructable history. The distinction allows a coherent account while keeping the degree of certainty visible.

## 4.4 From a pass to a river system

After the frontier stages, the narrative turns towards river movement and residence. Barua describes rafts, travel on the Dihing, prayers for safety, and movement upstream and downstream. The account subsequently brings the following to Tipam, Abhaypur, and Habung.[^c4-b-valley] The transition matters: a group accustomed to moving between territories now sought the conditions for repeated harvests and a continuing court.

Gait gives Christian-era dates to several moves. He places a residence at Mungklang, associated with Abhaypur, in 1236, a move to Habung in 1240, later movement towards the Dikhu, and arrival at Simaluguri in 1246. Charaideo is established in his account in 1253.[^c4-g-stages] These dates supply the current narrative framework. They still need to be reconciled with the durations and cyclical years in the translated chronicle.

One discrepancy is already apparent. Gait says that the following spent two years at Habung, yet places departure after renewed floods in 1244 following an arrival in 1240. Barua’s English account refers to three harvests there.[^c4-duration] These statements cannot be made identical without an explanation of counting conventions, calendar conversion, or textual error. The present draft leaves the problem open.

The sequence itself has a clear historical significance even before every date is resolved. Foundation is represented as experimentation. Flooding makes a move necessary; population and proximity to other inhabitants affect the desirability of a place; river travel permits another attempt. A settled kingdom emerges from this process rather than appearing fully formed at the first crossing.

## 4.5 The limits of a heroic route

A modern retelling often gives a founding journey a clear destination from its first moment. The surviving account does not allow us to know all of Sukaphaa’s intentions in that way. Departure can be described, and the later establishment of the kingdom can be followed, without inventing a plan that anticipated centuries of expansion.

Nor should the book supply conversations, private thoughts, or scenes of departure that no consulted source records. Where a text gives a speech, its wording is still a transmitted literary representation, not a recording of an occasion. Where it provides numbers, the reader should be told whose numbers they are.

The route map will consequently need several kinds of marks: reasonably identified places, approximate associations, and unresolved names. The connection between those marks is a reconstruction from sources. It is not a surveyed itinerary or proof that every modern road follows the medieval path.

What can already be narrated is substantial. A royal following moved westward from an eastern Tai setting, encountered inhabited territories, crossed the frontier region, and sought a durable home within the valley. Its traditions remembered conflict alongside submission, sacred objects alongside provisions, and environmental setbacks alongside political success. The establishment of Charaideo belongs to the next stage of that history: the making of a court that could survive its founder.

# Chapter 5 — Sukaphaa and the Founding Kingdom

**Period:** 1228–1268, working chronology. **Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026.  
**Status:** opening research draft; early institutions and funerary attribution require further verification.

## 5.1 Charaideo and a continuing court

The founding tradition reaches a turning point when Sukaphaa establishes his court at Charaideo. Earlier residences had been abandoned or altered. The new centre is described as a town beside a small hill, with ceremonies marking its establishment. The conventional date used here is 1253, following Gait’s reconstruction.[^c5-foundation]

Barua’s translation supplies a ritual account of the occasion. It describes offerings to spirits, ceremonies near a tree, and the sacrifice of two horses, one to the north and one to the south.[^c5-ritual] These details place the new seat of rule within a sacred as well as a practical landscape. The court’s presence was represented through ritual relations, not simply through the occupation of land.

The passage gives no complete plan of the town and no secure description of its population. It should not be expanded into an imagined capital with palaces, streets, workshops, and defensive works assigned to particular locations without evidence. The historical point presently supported is the remembered establishment of a continuing royal centre after a series of moves.

Charaideo’s later importance strengthens the need to separate phases. A site associated with a dynasty over centuries may preserve structures and practices from different reigns. Its lasting sacred position does not make every surviving feature a construction of Sukaphaa’s lifetime. Material evidence and later royal traditions must be dated individually.

The settlement sequence also prevents the foundation from being reduced to a single act. The court at Charaideo followed years of movement, residence, and relations with other inhabitants. It was the outcome of that process. It then became a point around which further political relationships could be organised.

## 5.2 Moran and Borahi relations

Gait’s narrative names Moran and Borahi rulers and describes their defeat, followed by conciliation and intermarriage. It presents the incorporation of local people as central to the growth of the founding kingdom.[^c5-local] This is an important source report. Its compressed formulation should not be read as the complete experience of all members of either community.

Conquest, marriage, service, and alliance create different relationships. A chief might recognise a new ruler while households continued familiar practices. A family might enter a royal service arrangement without every neighbouring settlement changing in the same way. Intermarriage could connect influential households without dissolving a whole people into a single identity.

Barua’s edition preserves lists of families and functions, including groups associated with household and practical services.[^c5-service] These records deserve close attention because they make the kingdom’s operation dependent on people beyond the royal line. Food, craft, transport, care of animals, ritual work, and the maintenance of a court could not be supplied by royal ancestry alone.

The lists also require chronological caution. Several genealogical passages move across reigns and explain later positions through ancestral beginnings. A statement placing a family within the founder’s world may be evidence of that family’s remembered relationship to foundation, while leaving the exact date of a service arrangement uncertain.

Local agency must therefore remain visible in the questions asked of these sources. What did existing residents supply? How were chiefs and families rewarded or constrained? Which relationships continued outside the court? The sources assembled so far permit only partial answers. They support a history of encounters and incorporation, not a claim that local people were passive material from which the king made a state.

## 5.3 Authority, officers, land, and labour

Gait associates the beginning of the kingdom with two leading offices, the Burhagohain and Borgohain.[^c5-officers] These offices are important to the later political history, but their founding functions should not be described by importing the whole administrative order of the seventeenth or eighteenth century.

The distinction matters especially for the Borbarua and Borphukan. Their later prominence does not establish their existence in Sukaphaa’s court. Likewise, detailed arrangements for labour groups and service land need dated evidence before they are assigned to the thirteenth century. The book will explain institutional development as a process, retaining differences between early leadership, later reform, and the terminology supplied by an editor.

The migration narrative itself describes representatives left in charge of places as the following moved on.[^c5-representatives] These reports indicate a remembered effort to preserve connections beyond the ruler’s immediate location. They do not prove that each such place had the same form of administration or that every appointment endured indefinitely.

Land and labour are inseparable from this question. A court requires production sufficient to support people engaged in activities other than immediate food cultivation. The early narrative’s concern with harvests and settlement makes that need visible. The exact mechanism by which provisions and labour were obtained is less fully described in the passages consulted.

It is therefore reasonable to ask how royal authority coordinated cultivation, service, and defence. It is premature to answer with a complete later paik-system diagram labelled 1228. The difference between a useful analytical question and an established institutional fact must remain clear.

Agricultural knowledge also needs a more exact history than the assertion that one founder introduced rice to Assam. Newcomers and residents may have brought different practices into contact. Identifying a technique, tracing its diffusion, and showing how a court organised its use are distinct research tasks. Political foundation alone establishes none of them.

## 5.4 Kingship and remembered order

The founder’s authority was represented through ancestry, followers, ritual objects, and relations with other rulers. The ceremonies at Charaideo connect the court to spirits and place. The genealogical traditions connect the dynasty to a larger sacred and regional order. Together, they show how later historical memory made its beginnings meaningful.

Barua’s miscellaneous account renders the name of the new country as Mungdunshukham and provides an English gloss relating it to golden gardens.[^c5-country] The gloss should be retained as a translated source expression. A definitive etymology requires linguistic examination; the English phrase alone cannot settle the meaning and historical use of each component.

Gait also records the sending of gifts to rulers treated as related or brother rulers to the east.[^c5-gifts] This places the new court within a remembered network extending beyond its local settlements. Its eastern connections did not necessarily disappear when Sukaphaa entered the valley. The exact chronology, recipients, and political meaning of these exchanges need fuller comparison before a complete diplomatic register can be offered.

Historical record keeping belongs to the same broader problem of continuity. Surviving buranjis preserve early names and sequences, but the copies and editions now available stand at a distance from the founding reign. The statement that the dynasty valued its historical past is supported by the corpus. The assertion that a particular surviving passage was written at Sukaphaa’s command requires evidence about that passage’s production.

The distinction does not diminish the record. It makes possible a more precise history of how a kingdom remembered its founder, transmitted accounts of authority, and connected descendants to the beginning.

## 5.5 Death and the test of succession

The working chronology places Sukaphaa’s death in 1268. Barua’s translation gives that equivalent date and then records the succession of his son Suteuphaa. Gait follows the same sequence.[^c5-death] Their agreement remains agreement within a partly shared source lineage, but it provides the initial chronological framework for the next chapter.

Barua also states that Sukaphaa reigned for forty-one full years. Simple subtraction between 1228 and 1268 gives forty, reminding us that regnal durations may use different counting conventions or may contain textual problems.[^c5-duration] This draft does not resolve the discrepancy by silently changing a date. Calendar and regnal counting need to be examined together.

Royal death raises a further question about Charaideo’s funerary history. The dynasty’s association with the site is clear as a subject of research. Assigning a particular surviving mound to the founder, reconstructing every element of his funeral, or describing grave contents requires evidence beyond the short death notice. A later customary practice cannot automatically be projected back to 1268.

The first succession matters because it carried authority beyond the personal following of a founder. The sources record a son taking the throne. They do not give a complete account of every negotiation that made this possible. The transition should be narrated without inventing unanimous consent, private instructions, or a settled constitutional rule that the evidence has not established.

By the end of the founding reign, the tradition describes a court rooted at Charaideo, relations with surrounding communities, senior leaders, sacred practices, and connections beyond the valley. These are the elements from which the next reigns must be understood. The durability of the dynasty would depend on how successors maintained and changed those relationships.
