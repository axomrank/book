# Chapter 2 — The Brahmaputra Valley on the Eve of 1228

**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; political zones remain approximate.

## 2.1 Rivers and the choice of a home

The settlement history attributed to Sukaphaa makes geography an active part of the foundation story. Rivers carry his followers towards possible homes. Flooding helps explain why they leave them. A place that permits arrival does not necessarily permit a durable court. The early narrative is a sequence of adjustments to water, food, neighbours, and the practical demands of staying.

Barua’s translated chronicle describes movement along the Dihing, residence at Tipam, a stay at Abhaypur, and a subsequent move to Habung. Floodwater recurs in the explanation of these changes. The account also represents the inhabitants and their harvests as matters important to the migrating group. Later, the court is established beside a hill at Charaideo.[^c2-b-route] These passages do not provide a modern environmental survey. They preserve a historical tradition in which the success of settlement depends on understanding a landscape.

The valley should accordingly be imagined as more than an expanse of cultivable ground. A river can connect settlements and separate them. A crossing can become a point of negotiation. A flooded tract can be temporarily unusable while another nearby site remains productive. Movement between hill passages, tributaries, and the main river links different environments and different bodies of local knowledge.

The chronicle’s account does not establish the exact medieval course of every river. Modern place names and channels cannot simply be placed on a map and labelled “1228”. Where identification remains uncertain, the map accompanying this history will mark an approximate association or leave a location unresolved. The reader should be able to distinguish the route transmitted by a source from the geographical reconstruction made by an editor.

## 2.2 A political landscape with several centres

Sukaphaa did not enter a region lacking political history. Earlier kingdoms, local chiefs, religious establishments, and cultivated settlements formed the background to the new dynasty. Nor was the whole valley governed by a single authority at the moment of arrival. A history of Ahom expansion must begin with the plurality of that setting.

Gait’s account describes Chutiya power towards the east and Kachari power across substantial tracts south of the Brahmaputra, with Bhuyan chiefs in other areas. It places Moran and Borahi communities within the region of the earliest Ahom settlements.[^c2-g-zones] This supplies an initial orientation, but its schematic arrangement should not be turned into an exact boundary map for one particular year. A later chronicle can preserve the names of neighbouring powers without demonstrating precisely where their authority ended in 1228.

Kamarupa and Kamata also require careful handling. These names belong to changing political and geographical usages. They do not denote two timeless areas whose boundaries can be carried unchanged through the whole medieval period. Gait’s discussion itself shows the difficulty of relating a title, a place name, and the territory of a ruler.[^c2-g-kamata] The chapters that follow will attach each diplomatic or military encounter to its own period, rather than treat every mention of a western ruler as evidence for the same enduring state.

This approach changes the meaning of “expansion”. The extension of Ahom influence might take the form of a defeated ruler, a marriage, a tribute arrangement, the installation of an officer, or the movement of a population. These are different relationships. A campaign that reached a place does not by itself establish that the court thereafter administered it continuously.

## 2.3 Communities with histories of their own

Moran, Borahi, Chutiya, Kachari or Dimasa, and Bhuyan histories cannot be reduced to the roles assigned to them in the rise of the Ahom court. The court’s records are indispensable, but their field of vision is political and selective. When they describe another people as conquered or incorporated, they report a relationship from one side of it.

The names themselves need explanation. A designation used by a medieval or later chronicler may cover a ruler, a cluster of settlements, a political following, or a community. It need not coincide exactly with the meaning attached to the same name today. Historical discussion should preserve the name in its source while explaining the uncertainty of the correspondence.

Gait’s treatment of the Bhuyans offers a particular caution. He describes variation in the lists and traditions attached to the celebrated group of twelve chiefs.[^c2-g-bhuyan] The number should therefore not be used to invent a permanent council of exactly twelve members governing a fixed territory throughout the period. Individual families, claims to rank, and changing combinations of authority require separate investigation.

The treatment of Chutiya origins raises a related problem. Gait records traditions of ancestry and early rulers, but the presence of a genealogy does not make its entire sequence independently dated history.[^c2-g-chutiya] These traditions deserve a place because they show how a political past was remembered. They also require comparison with Chutiya perspectives and with material or documentary evidence that is independent of the Ahom conquest narrative.

Recovering local agency means following the relationships described in the sources closely. Who remained in place? Who supplied labour or food? Which chiefs negotiated? Which families entered the court? Where these questions cannot yet be answered, the gap should remain visible. A smooth sentence about the incorporation of a whole people can conceal many different experiences.

## 2.4 Cultivation, exchange, and existing knowledge

Food production is fundamental to the settlement story, but the relationship between migration and agricultural change is contested. It is easy to make a dramatic claim that the Ahoms introduced cultivation to an empty or unproductive valley. The sources consulted for this draft do not justify such a claim.

The translated buranji describes Sukaphaa’s followers living through successive harvests at Habung.[^c2-b-route] Whatever the exact historical circumstances behind that notice, the narrative itself does not describe a land without food production. To turn it into such a landscape would require overlooking the people already present in the story.

Modern discussion also cautions against a simple account of a single agricultural beginning. Hemanta Kumar Saud’s 2018 thesis notes uncertainty about the relative importance of wet and dry rice, and of permanent and shifting cultivation, in the pre-Ahom valley.[^c2-saud] That observation does not settle which community introduced which technique in a particular locality. It makes the question more precise: crop, method, area, period, and evidence must be identified separately.

Rice cultivation, water management, ironworking, livestock, craft production, and exchange should therefore be investigated as practices with histories. A new ruling group may bring knowledge, organise its use on a different scale, or learn from existing residents. These possibilities are not mutually exclusive. The account of agricultural change must be built from evidence, rather than from an assumption that political arrival necessarily marks technological invention.

Religion and language deserve the same attention to coexistence. The beginnings of a dynasty do not erase the practices of the people around it. The source base presently assembled is strongest on elite relations and movement. A fuller account of the valley’s everyday life before 1228 needs inscriptions, archaeology, historical linguistics, and research outside the dynastic narrative.

## 2.5 Geography and the possibility of a kingdom

The early settlement tradition suggests that founding a kingdom required several problems to be solved together. A court needed a location. Its following needed food. Its leaders needed working relations with neighbours. Routes had to connect settlements without exposing movement entirely to flooding or resistance. These are analytical conclusions drawn from the narrative, not a list of policies recorded in Sukaphaa’s own words.

The repeated moves are significant because they give foundation a duration. Arrival in 1228 and the establishment of Charaideo in the conventional year 1253 are different moments. Between them lies a remembered process of searching, settling, leaving, and settling again.[^c2-g-settlement] The story is less intelligible if that process is compressed into the instant of a border crossing.

The possibilities were also unequal. People who knew the land possessed information indispensable to newcomers. Migrants who arrived with a leader and a following possessed their own means of coordination. The kingdom developed through encounters between such resources, including conflict. Geography shaped the conditions under which these encounters occurred; it did not dictate a single inevitable result.

The next chapter follows the traditions carried into this landscape. Before examining the crossing of the Patkai, it asks what the surviving sources mean when they connect the dynasty to a wider Tai world and to royal ancestors beyond the valley.
