# Chapter 4 — Across the Patkai: Arrival in 1228

**Version:** 0.1, 10 October 2026. **Status:** opening research draft; route, calendar conversions, and population figures require further checks.

## 4.1 A migration preserved in several forms

The conventional beginning of Ahom rule in Assam is 1228. That date gives the reader a clear point of orientation. The story attached to it is more complicated than the arrival of a ruler at a border. It includes departure, movement through several territories, encounters with local powers, river travel, and the search for places where the following could remain.

Gait’s account places Sukaphaa’s departure in 1215 and his arrival in the Khamjang region in 1228, after a journey of thirteen years. It gives a following of nine thousand people, eight nobles, two elephants, and three hundred horses.[^c4-g-numbers] These are figures reported by a historian working from chronicle translations. They are not an independently verified census of migrants.

Barua’s edition allows a closer view of one numerical feature. The migration passage describes three thousand cooking pots, each said to hold food for three men, alongside named elephants and three hundred horses.[^c4-b-pots] The arithmetic yields nine thousand portions. It does not by itself settle the number of people actually present, whether all were counted in the same way, or the age and sex composition of the following. Gait’s further suggestion about the proportion of adult men must remain his inference.

Precision in a narrative can be memorable and politically meaningful. It does not remove the need to investigate how a figure was transmitted. The final book should preserve these numbers as source reports while resisting the temptation to calculate a modern demographic profile from them.

## 4.2 The frontier was inhabited

The passage towards the valley took Sukaphaa through territories with their own inhabitants and chiefs. Barua’s translation names stages and associates some of them with resistance, submission, or the placement of a representative. Its account does not describe an empty corridor waiting for a new ruler.[^c4-b-frontier]

The text also contains severe violence attributed to Sukaphaa’s forces, including destruction of villages, captivity, and an atrocity involving coerced consumption of human flesh. These reports should neither be removed to protect a heroic image nor presented as independently established detail without qualification.[^c4-b-violence] They belong to the chronicle’s representation of conquest and fear. Comparison with other witnesses and a review of the translation are needed before the event is reconstructed more closely.

The historical term Naga, as used in these accounts, also needs care. It must not be treated as a precise identification of every present-day community in the region. A source may use a broad designation for different settlements or peoples. Its language tells us something about the chronicler’s categories; it does not give permission to generalise the character or conduct of their descendants.

Barua’s miscellaneous account presents another aspect of the movement. It describes people offering tribute, being permitted to remain, and some joining the following.[^c4-b-tribute] These statements cannot automatically be assigned to every named stage of the more detailed route. They nevertheless show that the transmitted story contains several forms of encounter: fighting, submission, continued local residence, and recruitment.

Migration and conquest are therefore not entirely separate categories in the source narrative. A moving royal following could seek passage, provisions, recognition, and authority at the same time. What happened at a particular place must be examined on its own evidence.

## 4.3 What 1228 can establish

The date 1228 anchors the conventional regnal chronology. It should not be made to prove that a mature kingdom, with its later territorial reach and administrative offices, came into existence that year. Arrival and durable institutional development are different historical questions.

Gait regarded the chronology from Sukaphaa’s entry onwards as much firmer than the divine and ancestral narratives preceding it.[^c4-g-boundary] That judgement helped organise subsequent histories. It is still necessary to check the events attached to individual years. A chronological framework can be useful even where details within it remain uncertain.

Calendar conversion is one source of difficulty. Barua’s translated passages retain named cyclical years and occasionally give an equivalent Christian-era date. The migration account does not convert every stage into a year that a modern reader can immediately compare with Gait’s sequence.[^c4-b-years] Conversion must therefore be examined as part of the research, rather than supplied casually from an unreferenced online timeline.

This book uses 1228 as a working beginning for the dynasty’s rule in Assam. It treats the route towards it as a transmitted and partly reconstructable history. The distinction allows a coherent account while keeping the degree of certainty visible.

## 4.4 From a pass to a river system

After the frontier stages, the narrative turns towards river movement and residence. Barua describes rafts, travel on the Dihing, prayers for safety, and movement upstream and downstream. The account subsequently brings the following to Tipam, Abhaypur, and Habung.[^c4-b-valley] The transition matters: a group accustomed to moving between territories now sought the conditions for repeated harvests and a continuing court.

Gait gives Christian-era dates to several moves. He places a residence at Mungklang, associated with Abhaypur, in 1236, a move to Habung in 1240, later movement towards the Dikhu, and arrival at Simaluguri in 1246. Charaideo is established in his account in 1253.[^c4-g-stages] These dates supply the current narrative framework. They still need to be reconciled with the durations and cyclical years in the translated chronicle.

One discrepancy is already apparent. Gait says that the following spent two years at Habung, yet places departure after renewed floods in 1244 following an arrival in 1240. Barua’s English account refers to three harvests there.[^c4-duration] These statements cannot be made identical without an explanation of counting conventions, calendar conversion, or textual error. The present draft leaves the problem open.

The sequence itself has a clear historical significance even before every date is resolved. Foundation is represented as experimentation. Flooding makes a move necessary; population and proximity to other inhabitants affect the desirability of a place; river travel permits another attempt. A settled kingdom emerges from this process rather than appearing fully formed at the first crossing.

## 4.5 The limits of a heroic route

A modern retelling often gives a founding journey a clear destination from its first moment. The surviving account does not allow us to know all of Sukaphaa’s intentions in that way. Departure can be described, and the later establishment of the kingdom can be followed, without inventing a plan that anticipated centuries of expansion.

Nor should the book supply conversations, private thoughts, or scenes of departure that no consulted source records. Where a text gives a speech, its wording is still a transmitted literary representation, not a recording of an occasion. Where it provides numbers, the reader should be told whose numbers they are.

The route map will consequently need several kinds of marks: reasonably identified places, approximate associations, and unresolved names. The connection between those marks is a reconstruction from sources. It is not a surveyed itinerary or proof that every modern road follows the medieval path.

What can already be narrated is substantial. A royal following moved westward from an eastern Tai setting, encountered inhabited territories, crossed the frontier region, and sought a durable home within the valley. Its traditions remembered conflict alongside submission, sacred objects alongside provisions, and environmental setbacks alongside political success. The establishment of Charaideo belongs to the next stage of that history: the making of a court that could survive its founder.
