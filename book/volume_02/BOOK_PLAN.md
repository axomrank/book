# Volume 2 — War, Power and Renewal

Series: **From Sukaphaa to Gomdhar Konwar: A History of the Ahom Dynasty**.

**Scope:** Main political chronology: 1603–1769.

**Chapters:** 17–34.

These chapter briefs are extracted from the [series master plan](../Ahom_Dynasty_Book_Plan_1228_to_Gomdhar_Konwar.md). Shared evidence rules, production recommendations, appendices, and source strategy remain in that master. It is the authority if a volume extract becomes out of date; update both when revising a brief.

### Chapter 17. Pratap Singha and the Reorganisation of Power

**Coverage:** 1603–1641; working chronology.

17.1. Accession of Susenghphaa/Pratap Singha and the priorities of the reign.

17.2. Momai Tamuli and other leading administrators; the evolution of the Borbarua and Borphukan offices.

17.3. Reorganisation of labour, settlements, provincial administration, and military command.

17.4. Royal policy towards religious institutions and the changing political economy.

17.5. Court politics, frontier policy, succession, and the limits of claims assigning every institution to one reformer.

**Planned supporting material:** An administration chart distinguishing earlier institutions from later reforms.

### Chapter 18. The First Major Ahom–Mughal War Cycle

**Coverage:** Approximately 1615–1639.

18.1. Mughal expansion through Bengal, Koch Hajo, local allies, and frontier ambitions.

18.2. Campaign-by-campaign accounts of early fighting, including engagements such as Samdhara where supported.

18.3. Balinarayan and regional resistance; commanders, forts, river crossings, and supply.

18.4. Compare Ahom chronicles with Mirza Nathan’s Baharistan-i-Ghaybi and other Persian accounts.

18.5. The Asurar Ali settlement, its terms, frontier implications, and later violations.

**Planned supporting material:** A campaign sequence and side-by-side source comparison.

### Chapter 19. The Short Reigns after Pratap Singha

**Coverage:** 1641–1648; working chronology.

19.1. Suramphaa/Jayaditya Singha and Sutingphaa: individual reign and household dossiers.

19.2. Accession, ministers, royal disputes, illness traditions, deposition, and succession.

19.3. Continuity and change in administration, religious patronage, and frontier policy.

19.4. What chronicles record and what later authors infer from sparse material.

19.5. How short reigns affected the court before Jayadhwaj Singha’s accession.

**Planned supporting material:** A two-reign chronology with a distinction between facts and explanations.

### Chapter 20. Jayadhwaj Singha before the Invasion

**Coverage:** 1648–1663; focus before 1662.

20.1. Sutamla/Jayadhwaj Singha: accession, officers, household, and religious patronage.

20.2. Western expansion, Mughal weakness, Guwahati, and the disputed frontier.

20.3. Military and administrative preparations, alliances, and intelligence.

20.4. Vaishnava affiliations and the coexistence of different religious practices.

20.5. The political circumstances that preceded Mir Jumla’s campaign.

**Planned supporting material:** A frontier map and a royal-and-ministerial profile.

### Chapter 21. Mir Jumla’s Invasion

**Coverage:** 1662–1663.

21.1. Mughal objectives, commanders, routes, boats, artillery, and logistics.

21.2. Ahom defensive decisions, retreat, the occupation of the capital, and resistance.

21.3. Monsoon, disease, supply failure, communications, and civilian experiences.

21.4. Comparison of Ahom records, Persian narratives, and later historical writing.

21.5. Separate attested movements from dramatic reconstructions of conversations or motives.

**Planned supporting material:** A season-by-season campaign map and logistics diagram.

### Chapter 22. Ghilajharighat and Ramani Gabharu

**Coverage:** 1663 and its aftermath.

22.1. Treaty negotiations, payments, hostages, boundaries, and obligations as recorded in relevant texts.

22.2. The political and economic consequences for the royal household and kingdom.

22.3. Ramani Gabharu: kinship, transfer to the Mughal court, later life, and limits of the evidence.

22.4. Diplomatic correspondence and marriage as instruments of imperial politics.

22.5. Jayadhwaj’s death, succession, and how the settlement was remembered.

**Planned supporting material:** A treaty-clause comparison and a documented biography of Ramani Gabharu.

### Chapter 23. Chakradhwaj Singha and the Recovery of Guwahati

**Coverage:** 1663–1670; working chronology.

23.1. Supangmung/Chakradhwaj Singha: accession and the decision to resist Mughal demands.

23.2. Lachit Borphukan, Atan Burhagohain, ministers, officers, and collective command.

23.3. Recruitment, shipbuilding, fortification, supplies, and the recovery campaign.

23.4. Recapture of Guwahati and rebuilding of defensive positions.

23.5. Assess royal speeches, command legends, and claims about individual responsibility against their earliest sources.

**Planned supporting material:** A recovery campaign map and biographies of the command team.

### Chapter 24. Alaboi: Defeat and Its Consequences

**Coverage:** 1669.

24.1. Political and military pressure surrounding the confrontation.

24.2. Terrain, deployment, command choices, and the limits of tactical reconstruction.

24.3. The battle sequence and variations in casualty figures.

24.4. Consequences for strategy, morale, fortification, negotiations, and civilian communities.

24.5. How Alaboi entered memory and why defeat belongs alongside victory in a complete history.

### Chapter 25. Saraighat: War on the Brahmaputra

**Coverage:** 1671.

25.1. Ram Singh’s campaign, negotiations, river defences, and the strategic position of Guwahati.

25.2. Lachit, Atan, naval officers, soldiers, labourers, allies, and opposing commanders.

25.3. Land engagements, naval manoeuvres, supply, illness traditions, and battle chronology.

25.4. Udayaditya Singha’s reign during the battle and the wider political setting.

25.5. Victory, its limits, subsequent frontier developments, and the source history of famous anecdotes.

**Coverage:** 1228–1769; thematic synthesis.

34.1. Dedicated sections for Kachari/Dimasa, Jaintia, Koch polities, Manipur, Tripura, and relevant eastern connections.

34.2. Relations with Naga, Mising/Miri, Mishmi, Khamti, Singpho, Bhutanese, and other communities where period evidence exists.
