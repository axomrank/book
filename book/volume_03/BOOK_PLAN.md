# Volume 3 — Society, Collapse and Resistance

Series: **From Sukaphaa to Gomdhar Konwar: A History of the Ahom Dynasty**.

**Scope:** Thematic history across the kingdom’s lifespan; late political history through Gomdhar Konwar’s 1828 uprising, with only explicitly bounded aftermath.

**Chapters:** 35–60.

These chapter briefs are extracted from the [series master plan](../Ahom_Dynasty_Book_Plan_1228_to_Gomdhar_Konwar.md). Shared evidence rules, production recommendations, appendices, and source strategy remain in that master. It is the authority if a volume extract becomes out of date; update both when revising a brief.

### Chapter 35. Kingship, Government, and Justice

**Coverage:** 1228–1828; developments by period.

35.1. Royal succession, legitimacy, coronation, council politics, and limits on royal power.

35.2. Burhagohain, Borgohain, Borpatrogohain, Borbarua, Borphukan, and subordinate officers.

35.3. Provincial, local, frontier, household, and specialised departments and offices.

35.4. Judicial practice, petitions, customary rules, punishments, property disputes, and evidence gaps.

35.5. Appointments, service, gifts, royal orders, land grants, and institutional change over six centuries.

**Planned supporting material:** A dated administration diagram and an office glossary.

### Chapter 36. The Paik System: Service, Land, and Obligation

**Coverage:** Evolution across the Ahom period.

36.1. Origins and successive reforms; distinguish early practices from later standardisation.

36.2. Paik categories, gots, khels, service rotation, officer hierarchy, and exemptions.

36.3. Links between land, cultivation, military duty, transport, construction, and craft labour.

36.4. Household burdens, displacement, desertion, unequal obligations, and dealings with religious institutions.

36.5. Breakdown, adaptation, and the system’s relationship to rebellion and colonial transition.

**Planned supporting material:** A worked service-cycle example labelled with its historical period.

### Chapter 38. Land, Agriculture, and the Rural Economy

### Chapter 40. Communities, Migration, and Social Hierarchy

**Coverage:** 1228–1828.

40.2. Ahomisation, Assamese-language expansion, incorporation, intermarriage, and continuing distinct identities.

40.3. Caste, rank, clan, profession, noble households, service groups, slavery, and dependency.

40.4. Muslim communities, immigrants, captives, religious networks, and frontier mobility.

### Chapter 41. Women, Family, and Political Agency

**Coverage:** 1228–1828.

41.1. Royal women, political marriages, succession, property, household authority, and court patronage.

41.2. Case studies of Mula Gabharu, Ramani Gabharu, Joymoti, Phuleswari, Ambika, Sarbeswari, and Kuranganayani.

41.3. Ordinary women’s cultivation, weaving, trade, ritual, household labour, and experiences of conflict.

41.4. Marriage, kinship, inheritance, sexuality, widowhood, captivity, and status as far as evidence permits.

41.5. Identify the limits imposed by male court authors and avoid presenting exceptional royal lives as universal experience.

### Chapter 42. Religion, Ritual, and Religious Institutions

42.1. Tai-Ahom cosmology, ancestor traditions, ritual specialists, ceremonies, and changing royal practice.

42.2. Shaiva, Shakta, Vaishnava, and other religious traditions and their regional institutions.
